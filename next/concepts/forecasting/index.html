Concepts

Forecasting

Napwake learns each workload's daily and weekly pattern of demand with a Holt-Winters double seasonal model. It forecasts demand from it.

For your assistantView .mdAsk Claude ↗ (opens claude.ai)Ask ChatGPT ↗ (opens chatgpt.com)
It always learns

For every managed workload, whatever autoResume says. With ask, it learns the hours it’s paused behind the doorman from whether people woke it. Once confident, it holds off a pause when it expects demand in the hour under way or the next.

With autoResume

It also wakes a paused workload 15 minutes before an hour it expects busy. Turn it on any time. The forecast has been learning all along, and wakes ahead once it acts.

With pauseEarly

Off by default. It also pauses an idle workload before idleAfter runs out.

It does so when it’s sure the hours ahead are quiet, and a request would wake it, never with ask. Otherwise the activity clock decides every pause.

The forecast learns the shape of a day, busy in business hours and quiet at night. It also learns how each weekday departs from that shape. It learns the two apart, from each hour’s CPU utilisation. The simulator, which runs the operator’s own engine and rules, measured every figure on this page.

Phase lifecycle

Section titled “Phase lifecycle”

The forecast earns its way to acting, phase by phase.

  1. Observingits first week
  2. DailySuggestingscoring each day
  3. DailyActiveacts on the day
  4. WeeklySuggestingscoring each week
  5. FullyActiveacts on both
Phase Reached when What it does
Observing No day of forecasts is scored yet Collects data, its first week. An hour of the week is scored from the second time it’s seen. No predictions yet
DailySuggesting Every hour of the day is observed, and a day of forecasts is scored Scores daily confidence, without acting on it
DailyActive Daily confidence reaches the threshold Predictions drive decisions. Weekly patterns are still learning
WeeklySuggesting Every hour of the week is observed at least once Earns weekly confidence
FullyActive Weekly confidence reaches the threshold Daily and weekly patterns both drive decisions
Case What it means
The gates They count hours of the calendar, not data points. A workload observed only from 9 to 5 hasn’t shown its nights, however long it’s watched
A workload paused every night with neither the doorman nor a Prometheus activity query Its nights are never observed (What an hour records), so it never reaches DailySuggesting. Route it through the doorman for autoResume and the forecast’s veto. That works with request, or with ask, whose paused hours there are learned from whether people woke it. A workload no Service routes requests to, such as a queue worker, needs an activity query that sees its demand. A query of its queue’s length is one
The threshold The forecast setting’s, and a change applies at the next reconcile. A phase is earned at the threshold and lost 5 points below it. So confidence hovering there doesn’t switch the forecast on and off every hour
status.prediction A phase per season. dailyPhase and weeklyPhase are each Observing, Suggesting or Active, next to dailyConfidence and weeklyConfidence. DailyActive is dailyPhase: Active with weeklyPhase: Observing
After a regime change The phase shown, there and in napwake_prediction_phase, is the one the forecast acts in. Left at WeeklySuggesting with nothing scored, it shows DailySuggesting, until a day of its forecasts is scored and it drives decisions again

Forecast setting

Section titled “Forecast setting”

How soon the forecast acts is one setting, eager, balanced (the default) or cautious.

Set it on With
An opted-in workload or its namespace The annotation napwake.dev/forecast
Every opted-in workload The Helm value defaults.forecast
A ManagedWorkload you write spec.prediction.mode
A ManagedWorkload, with a threshold of your own spec.prediction.confidence, from 50 to 100, which overrides the setting’s. A label-created ManagedWorkload keeps it when its annotations are applied again
Setting Acts at a confidence of What it trades
eager 60% Acts on less regular patterns that balanced waits out. Wakes a workload for nothing more often
balanced 75% The default. Acts on a weekday pattern after 8 days. Almost never wakes a steadily used workload for nothing
cautious 85% Acts only on patterns it forecasts well. For workloads costly to keep awake for nothing, such as one on GPUs
A threshold below 60 Gains a morning or so on the most irregular patterns, for two more hours woken for nothing
A threshold above 85 Loses mornings about as fast as it saves hours

A wrong forecast costs a little money, never availability. It only wakes a workload early or keeps it up longer. A paused workload still wakes on a request through the doorman, or with ask on a person’s click. So the choice is between mornings ready and hours awake for nothing. Twenty simulated workloads averaged this over eight weeks of loosely kept office hours. People arrived up to an hour either side of 09:10, and were away one weekday in four.

Setting Mornings ready, of 30 Hours woken for nothing
eager 10 14
balanced 8 8
cautious 4 5

On steadily kept office hours, every setting acts after 8 days. Every morning after is ready, and none wakes the workload on a weekend. The settings differ only on patterns the forecast can’t get right every day.

How long it takes

Days to DailyActive / FullyActive, learning live from a fresh start, at 40% of the CPU request in use and 1% otherwise. autoResume is on, behind the doorman. The queue worker is woken by a Prometheus activity query instead. The public preview is woken only by people, with wake: ask. “Never” means not within ten weeks.

Pattern When it acts
Any the forecast can get right At every setting, once it has scored a day of its second week. The first week is learned, not scored
Office hours On the Tuesday of the second week. The Monday before is the only morning after the first week that the first request wakes the workload. Every morning after is ready, Mondays included. Weekends stay asleep, since the first weekend taught the forecast they’re quiet
One it can’t get right every day, as in the third row A higher threshold waits for more evidence
Always busy, or always idle Acting changes nothing. One is never paused, and the other never woken or held awake by its forecast of no demand

Pausing early

Section titled “Pausing early”

With pauseEarly on, a workload idle for 20 minutes is paused without waiting out idleAfter, when all of these hold:

Condition What it takes
The forecast is acting, and sure the hours ahead are quiet Every hour until idleAfter would run out, and at least the hour under way and the next, is forecast below cpuThreshold. It’s judged as the veto and autoResume judge, at the same threshold. An unseen hour of the week can’t be called quiet. Nor can the hours after one far quieter than forecast, as on a holiday
A request would wake it Its wake.mode is Request, the default. A workload that asks before waking never pauses early, since a request doesn’t wake it. The doorman is on and has a Ready pod, and the last pause found nothing wrong with routing (WakeOnRequest). Every Service that selects the pods is one the doorman would route while paused, on every port outside napwake.dev/doorman-ignore-ports. None is headless or has a UDP or SCTP port. None keeps traffic on the caller’s node (internalTrafficPolicy: Local), or has another workload’s Ready pods behind it. Behind GKE container-native load balancing, it has the forwarder. At least one routes TCP, and their ports fit the doorman ports the workload and its namespace may hold
Nothing else holds it These hold an early pause, as they hold any. An active-until hold, awake dependents, and the hour Napwake waits after a GitOps tool undid its last pause

A wrong forecast then costs a request a wake, a cold start, never its answer. With ask, a wrong guess would cost a person a click, so a workload that asks waits out idleAfter.

Case What happens
Anything above doesn’t hold, or can’t be read The workload waits out idleAfter as usual, and the operator logs why
Services without a selector, and ExternalName Services Not checked, since nothing says which pods they reach
Dry-run An early pause is a would-be pause, measured like any other
The forecast setting A separate setting. It says how sure the forecast must be to act at all, and this one whether, once sure, it brings pauses forward. So a cautious forecast can pause early too
The hours of an early pause Behind the doorman, learned like any paused hour (What an hour records), so the forecast keeps learning

It’s off unless you set napwake.dev/pause-early: "true" on an opted-in workload or its namespace. The Helm value defaults.pauseEarly sets it for every opted-in workload, and spec.idlePolicy.pauseEarly: true on a ManagedWorkload you write.

An early pause has a PausedEarly event in place of IdleDetected, naming the hours it expects quiet. Until the workload wakes, status.activity.forecastQuietUntil holds when those hours end. kubectl napwake status says so in NEXT. The event comes first here, then the status line:

What it saves and costs

Section titled “What it saves and costs”

The pause comes once the hour people leave in is over, since that hour is learned as busy. On office hours ending at 17:30, it comes at 18:00. The activity clock would have paused at 18:29, an idleAfter after the last busy minute. The longer idleAfter, the more that saves. The cost is a cold start for anyone who comes in an hour forecast quiet, before idleAfter would have run out.

The simulator measures both at balanced, for a workload at 40% of its CPU request in use. It counts the weeks after the forecast acts. For a holiday that’s its week, and for a change of hours the two weeks after.

Workload Hours awake a week, without → with Extra cold starts a week
Busy on weekdays from 09:10 to 17:30, quiet nights and weekends 48.7 → 46.3 0
The same, with idleAfter: 2h 53.7 → 46.3 0
The same hours every day, weekends included 68.1 → 64.8 0
Office hours, people arriving and leaving up to 20 minutes either side and away one weekday in ten (the average of 20) 45.2 → 43.3 0
Office hours, people arriving and leaving up to an hour either side and away one weekday in four (the average of 20) 38.2 → 37.8 0
Office hours, the week of a holiday on a Wednesday 39.8 → 37.8 0
Office hours moving to 13:00 to 21:00, the two weeks after 56.0 → 54.7 0
Office hours, someone coming back for ten minutes 15 to 50 minutes after people left, one weekday in three (the average of 20) 50.6 → 48.6 0.6
Office hours, someone working in the evening too, one weekday in three, in three bursts with up to 25 minutes between them (the average of 20) 55.4 → 52.2 0.5
Always busy 168.0 → 168.0 0
Always idle 0.0 → 0.0 0

What it means
The default idleAfter of an hour Half an hour saved a weekday, about 5% of the hours awake. At two hours, an hour and a half a weekday
Learned patterns, holidays and changes of hours included No cold starts. Nothing is brought forward on a holiday, since the forecast doubts itself from the holiday’s first quiet hour. Nor on the first two days of new hours, while it relearns. And nobody comes back in the hours it pauses through
Loosely kept hours They save less. There are fewer days people leave to bring a pause forward from, and fewer evenings the forecast is sure of
Cold starts They come from use it can’t foresee within idleAfter of the last. Someone comes back after the others left, or works in the evening in bursts. Each gap over 20 minutes, in an hour learned as quiet, finds the workload paused
The 20 minutes At 10, evening work like that row’s costs three times the cold starts, for a quarter more of the hours saved. On office hours, any wait up to half an hour saves the same

Turn it on for workloads used to a pattern. There, an hour after the last use is an hour paid for nothing every day. Leave it off for one people come back to at odd hours, or set active-until while they do.

Learning from history

Section titled “Learning from history”

With a Prometheus URL (prometheus.url, --prometheus-url), a new ManagedWorkload’s forecast starts from the workload’s history. The operator reads up to the last four weeks of its CPU. It learns them in one go, as it would have live. Then it carries on live. A steady pattern acts from the first day. An irregular or short history goes on learning, through the same phase gates and confidence.

History How it’s read
An hour of history Measured as a live hour is. That’s the workload’s own containers’ usage against their requests, sidecars injected into its pods left out, at its busiest moment. Above cpuThreshold, it counts as at least twice cpuThreshold
Its pods Found by the names a Deployment or StatefulSet gives them, so old revisions’ pods are part of its history too
A Prometheus holding more than one cluster’s series, such as Thanos, Mimir or a shared one Set prometheus.selector (--prometheus-selector), such as cluster="staging". Its matchers are added to the query, so a same-named workload in another cluster isn’t read as this one
The query One range query per workload, a point an hour, of the busiest five-minute rate of container_cpu_usage_seconds_total in each hour. Prometheus works it out, and answers with at most 672 points
When it fails The operator asks for one workload at a time, and waits up to 30 seconds. A failing query is tried twice more, two minutes apart, before the forecast learns live only
A reconcile Never held up. The forecast learns live meanwhile, and takes up what was learned once it’s ready
How often Once per ManagedWorkload, when it’s new. One that has learned live keeps what it learned. Deleting and creating it again starts it afresh, from history again
What history can’t tell What it assumes
Hours none of its pods ran in Quiet, as an hour scaled to zero by something else is live. That includes hours Napwake paused it, if it managed the workload before. One paused behind the doorman or watched by an activity query is quiet anyway. One paused with neither, which live learning skips, can’t be told from an hour nobody used
Its CPU requests They’re today’s. If they changed over the four weeks, earlier hours are measured against the new ones
Use seen only in requests or activity annotations None, since that isn’t in Prometheus. So a light-use workload’s history looks quieter than its live hours will
Bursts Five-minute rates are smoother than the minute-by-minute readings live learning takes. A burst of a minute or two counts for less

status.prediction.history says what came of it, with an event once:

history.reason Event Meaning
Learned ForecastLearnedHistory Learned hours of history, from and to. The message says how much, such as the forecast learned from 4 weeks of the workload's CPU history in Prometheus
NoHistory ForecastLearningLive Prometheus has no CPU for its pods, as for a workload deployed with it. The forecast learns from now
NoCPURequests ForecastHistoryUnavailable (warning) Its containers request no CPU, so there’s nothing to measure its use against
QueryFailed ForecastHistoryUnavailable (warning) Prometheus didn’t answer three times. The message has its error
NoPrometheusURL none The operator has no Prometheus URL, so the forecast learns live from the start

kubectl napwake forecast shows what the forecast would make of your workloads’ history before you opt them in.

What an hour records

Section titled “What an hour records”

The forecast learns CPU utilisation, as a percentage of what the workload’s own containers request, in the pods reporting it. That’s the measure cpuThreshold is set in (Idle detection). The veto and the wake ahead compare the forecast with cpuThreshold directly. So a forecast that keeps an idle workload awake would also wake it once paused.

Each hour is fed in once it’s over, as the busiest moment seen in it. It’s recorded only if it was checked from its start to its end.

While the workload is The hour records
Awake The highest utilisation, from CPU read every minute. The first 15 minutes after a wake aren’t counted, whatever woke it, kubectl scale included. After that, an hour the activity clock sees it in use counts as at least twice cpuThreshold. That’s use from CPU above cpuThreshold, an activity annotation or a Prometheus query
Paused behind the doorman (WakeOnRequest=True), with wake.mode: Request No demand, while a check finds it still paused, since a request would have woken it. A request that wakes it counts as at least twice cpuThreshold, however little it then uses. So do a wake request and an activity annotation. That holds while its Prometheus activity queries can’t be evaluated. The hour is busy if a request woke it or a query found activity, and quiet otherwise
Paused behind the doorman, with wake.mode: Ask No demand, while a check finds it still paused, since someone who needed it would have woken it. A person’s wake counts as at least twice cpuThreshold. That’s a click on Wake it, a wake request or an activity annotation. So does a query finding activity
With Ask, while the doorman doesn’t route it, or its queries can’t be evaluated Demand is seen only if someone woke it, so the hour isn’t learned. Nobody can click then, and the doorman sees only people. So an outage of the doorman or of Prometheus can’t teach it quiet hours
With Ask, and no Service to route, such as a queue worker It learns from its queries while they can be evaluated, as with Request
Paused with a Prometheus activity query The query runs every minute, as while awake, and wakes the workload when it finds activity. A quiet check finds no demand, and activity counts as at least twice cpuThreshold. A query that can’t be evaluated never wakes it. Without the doorman, that hour isn’t recorded
Paused with neither watching, and wake.mode: Request Nothing. Demand it can’t see isn’t zero

A workload whose use stays under cpuThreshold, with no other source, is idle to Napwake while awake. So autoResume can’t learn its mornings. Lower cpuThreshold below its use, or add a source that sees it.

Why an hour records what it does
Rule Why
An hour records its peak The activity clock counts any minute above cpuThreshold as activity. An hour busy only for its last ten minutes is an hour the workload has to be awake for
The first 15 minutes after a wake don’t count Starting up isn’t demand. Counted, a wake ahead of a busy hour would teach the forecast that the hour before is busy too. It would wake the workload earlier every week
An hour in use counts as at least twice cpuThreshold A light-use workload’s use may show only in an annotation or a query. Without the floor, it would learn light use on mornings autoResume had it ready. On mornings people woke it, it would learn a request’s twice cpuThreshold. The forecast would then have it ready only every other morning
A waking request counts as twice cpuThreshold So the hour people arrive in is learned as busy. A wake request, an activity annotation and a click count the same
With ask, a paused hour behind the doorman nobody woke it in is quiet Only people wake it then. Someone who needed it would have clicked or asked
Only where people could have woken it So an outage can’t teach the forecast quiet hours
A query watches a paused workload That’s how a workload no Service routes requests to, such as a queue worker, learns its nights. It uses a query of its queue’s length
The hour under way is kept in memory After an operator restart or a leader change, that hour is skipped, unless it was only minutes old. It isn’t recorded from the part seen after

The model

Section titled “The model”

Holt-Winters keeps four components, and moves each a small step towards every hour it sees. A component’s smoothing parameter sets the size of its step.

Component What it represents Smoothing parameter
Level The mean demand α=0.01\alpha = 0.01α=0.01
Trend Whether demand is growing or shrinking β=0.001\beta = 0.001β=0.001
Daily components 24 offsets, one per hour of day, the shape of an average day γ1=0.1\gamma_1 = 0.1γ1​=0.1
Weekly components 168 offsets, one per hour of week, how each weekday departs from that shape γ2=0.5\gamma_2 = 0.5γ2​=0.5

Each hour, the model compares the hour’s utilisation Y(t)Y(t)Y(t) with what it predicted, for confidence scoring. Then it updates all four by exponential smoothing. It’s additive, since idle workloads spend much of their time at zero demand. A multiplicative model, which divides by the level and the seasonal factors, diverges there.

The forecast hhh hours ahead adds the level, hhh hours of trend, and that hour’s daily and weekly offsets. It’s never negative:

What the model does
A gap in the observations, from the operator being down or a workload paused with neither the doorman nor an activity query watching Skips it, since there’s no honest value to fill it with. Slots are wall-clock hours, so skipping one doesn’t shift what was learned
The trend across a gap Carries it, across a gap of at most a day
The first week Initialises the model, as the first season does in classical Holt-Winters. An hour of the week seen for the first time sets its weekly component, so its forecast is what was seen. The level, trend and daily components learn only from hours seen again
One week of office hours Forecasts the next week as the week it saw, weekends quiet
An hour of the week seen for the first time Doesn’t score it, since it had nothing to forecast it from. The forecast doesn’t act on an hour it hasn’t seen

Confidence scoring

Section titled “Confidence scoring”

Confidence is one minus the forecast’s error as a share of demand. It’s 1−WAPE1 - \text{WAPE}1−WAPE, the weighted absolute percentage error. That’s the total absolute error of the hourly forecasts over a window, divided by the total demand in it.

Term Meaning
A confidence of 75% The total error over the window is 25% of the demand in it
Zero demand Defined, unlike an average of per-hour percentage errors. An hour of zero demand forecast as zero isn’t counted as a perfect hour
The floor, n⋅fn \cdot fn⋅f fff is the workload’s mean hourly demand over about a week, and at least 1% of the CPU requested. So a quiet weekend day is judged against a typical day’s demand. A workload idling at a sliver of its request isn’t judged on its noise
Daily confidence Scored over the last 24 scored hours
Weekly confidence Scored over the last 168 scored hours, so a whole week of weekdays and weekend must be forecast well
A window not yet full No score until it is
An hour of the week seen for the first time Not scored, or judged an anomaly

Anomaly detection

Section titled “Anomaly detection”

Traffic patterns change, with a marketing campaign, a new feature or a change of season. Napwake detects these regime changes with z-score anomaly detection. An hour is an anomaly when its error is more than three standard deviations from the recent errors.

  1. Each hour, the signed prediction error e(t)=Y(t)−F(t)e(t) = Y(t) - F(t)e(t)=Y(t)−F(t) becomes a two-sided z-score. It uses an exponentially weighted mean μ\muμ and standard deviation σ\sigmaσ of recent errors, which remember about a week. A sudden surge and a sudden disappearance both count.

    z(t)=∣e(t)−μ∣max⁡(σ,f)z(t) = \frac{|e(t) - \mu|}{\max(\sigma, f)}z(t)=max(σ,f)∣e(t)−μ∣​

  2. If z(t)>3.0z(t) > 3.0z(t)>3.0, the hour is an anomaly. The model learns from it only up to 3 standard deviations, so a single spike isn’t learned as a pattern. An error that large in the same direction as the last one in its hour of the week isn’t an anomaly. It’s a weekly pattern, such as a Tuesday night batch job, and it’s learned in full.

  3. 3 or more anomalies within 24 observed hours are a regime change.

Idle detection and wake on request carry on throughout. A single deviant day can’t be told from the start of a new pattern until the next day shows which it was. So the forecast doesn’t guess. It costs the next morning, and nothing after.

Wall-clock alignment

Section titled “Wall-clock alignment”

Seasonal slots are keyed to wall-clock time, not a running counter. So Monday 9am always maps to the same slot, whatever the gaps.

Case What happens
The timezone The operator’s, --timezone or the Helm value timezone, UTC by default. Each workload records it in status.prediction.timeZone, and kubectl napwake status shows it
Daylight saving Business hours keep their slots when the clocks change. Set --timezone to its users’ zone, and a workload in New York or London is up at 08:45 local time every weekday. That holds through the change
Left at UTC The operator’s clock is an hour off its users’ after the change, so the forecast has to learn their mornings again. In the simulator, four mornings that week find the workload paused. It’s held awake for nothing on others, until about two weeks on
A timezone whose hours start at half or quarter past the hour in UTC, such as India’s Hours are counted on the local clock, and so are the 15 minutes autoResume wakes ahead
A workload paused for 6 hours without the doorman It keeps its alignment. The next observation goes into the right hour-of-day slot
An hour paused behind the doorman, asking before waking or not, or watched by an activity query Recorded as no demand. That’s how the model learns that nights and weekends are quiet

Persistence

Section titled “Persistence”

The learned state lives in the ManagedWorkload’s status, in status.prediction.state. It’s gzipped JSON, base64-encoded, about 2.5 KB, written with each hourly observation. No external database, persistent volume or ConfigMap is needed, and each workload has its own independent engine.

Case What happens
What it holds The model’s components, the scored errors, the anomaly statistics, and the weekly changes held for the next day. Whether the forecast doubts the hour under way. The last hour observed, and the demand last seen in each hour of the week, which startsActing is projected from
Operator restarts and leader changes The engine survives them. An hour observed before a restart isn’t observed twice, and the hour under way at a restart is skipped. Restarted daily, at a different hour each day, the operator loses those hours and nothing else. The forecast acts within hours of when it would have
A last observed hour more than an hour ahead of the operator’s clock Not trusted, since a wrong clock recorded it. The next hour is observed as usual, rather than refused until real time catches up
State that can’t be read, from an older version or a hand edit Discarded with a ForecastReset warning event. The engine starts learning again

Tuning

Section titled “Tuning”

The forecast setting, or spec.prediction.confidence in its place, is the only thing to tune. The smoothing parameters are fixed, the same for every workload, and can’t be configured.

Parameter Value What a higher value would do
α\alphaα 0.01 Level reacts faster to demand changes
β\betaβ 0.001 Trend detection is more aggressive
γ1\gamma_1γ1​ 0.1 Daily pattern adapts faster
γ2\gamma_2γ2​ 0.5 Weekly pattern adapts faster

Lower values give smoother, steadier forecasts, and higher ones a more reactive but noisier model. A weekly component updates once a week. So γ2\gamma_2γ2​ is high enough for a lasting change in a weekday’s hours to be half learned the week after. The first week sets each one outright (The model). The values were chosen by simulating office-hours, always-on, idle and noisy workloads.

Proving it

Section titled “Proving it”

A forecasting mistake would otherwise show only after weeks in a real cluster. Such mistakes are a wrong slot, a late wake or a false wake at the weekend. The repo’s simulator, internal/forecast/sim, runs the operator’s own forecast engine and decision rules. It runs them through simulated time a minute at a time, as the operator looks at a ManagedWorkload.

It covers the activity clock pausing, the veto, autoResume waking 15 minutes ahead, and the doorman or a query waking the workload. It covers the warm-up after a wake, each hour’s peak fed to the forecast, and restarts through the state saved in status. Months of traffic take milliseconds. A test runs six weeks of office hours through both the reconciler and the simulator. It finds the same wakes and pauses at the same minutes.

The tests run in CI on every change. Besides How long it takes, they check these:

Scenario What the tests check
Office hours Once learned, the workload is up at 08:45 every weekday, 25 minutes before people arrive at 09:10. It’s paused an hour after they leave, and left paused at weekends. At every setting, every morning from the day the forecast acts is ready, Mondays included, and it’s never still paused on one
Every day, always busy, always idle, light use seen only through an activity annotation Each acts as above. The light-use workload is ready every morning
Loosely kept hours Ready on most mornings once learned. Woken for nothing about twice a month, for an hour or two each time. The settings trade as Forecast setting says
A holiday, a Saturday at work, a permanent change of hours, a workload nobody uses any more, daylight saving in New York and London with and without --timezone, daily operator restarts As described above
Pauses with neither the doorman nor an activity query The forecast never acts
Pausing early On office hours, every weekday’s pause comes at 18:00. Mornings are as ready, and the same hours learned. Nothing is brought forward on a holiday or the first two days of new hours. Nobody finds the workload paused who wouldn’t have. Someone back after 18:00 finds it paused. Without the doorman, nothing is brought forward. The reconciler’s six weeks bring the same pauses forward at the same minutes. What it saves and costs is measured there too
A public preview only people wake, with wake: ask, paused every night and woken by someone at 09:05 on weekdays It acts after 8 days, as behind the doorman. From then, autoResume has it up at 08:45, before they come. One nobody wakes is learned as quiet, behind the doorman
A queue worker with no Service, woken by a query of its queue’s length, paused every night It acts after 8 days, as behind the doorman. From then, autoResume has it up before the morning’s messages
Learning from history Four weeks of office hours have the workload ready from the first morning. The projection made in the first week is within a day of when the forecast starts acting

Run the simulator with go run ./hack/forecastsim. It runs every scenario at every setting, and prints what it measured. These flags change what it runs:

Flag What it does
-scenario holiday -setting eager -days Shows one scenario day by day
-pause-early Brings pauses forward, counting the hours saved and the uses that then found the workload paused
-wake-on-request=false -wake-on-query Runs a scenario as a queue worker its activity query wakes
-wake-on-ask Runs a scenario as a workload only people wake, as wake: ask does
-history Replays a workload’s recorded CPU, as a percentage of its requests. It takes a Prometheus range query’s JSON, or CSV of time,percent rows, with steps at most seven minutes apart

On your own cluster, kubectl napwake forecast replays the forecast over each workload’s Prometheus history. It says whether the forecast would act if opted in today. For the last two weeks, it counts the mornings each workload would have been ready. It also counts how often one would have been woken for nothing.

Named, as kubectl napwake forecast api -n orders, it shows one workload’s forecast. For a workload Napwake manages, that’s its own forecast, from its status and saved state, whatever history Prometheus has. It shows the phase, the hours of the day and week seen, and the hours learned from history and live. It shows whether it’s acting or when it should, why, and the hours of the next day it expects busy. Then comes the replay of its history, day by day, when there’s enough of it.
