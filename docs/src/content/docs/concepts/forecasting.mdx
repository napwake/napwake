The forecast learns the shape of a day, busy in business hours and quiet at night. It also learns how each weekday departs from that shape. It learns the two apart, from each hour's CPU utilisation.

The [simulator](#proving-it), which runs the operator's own engine and rules, measured every figure on this page.

## Phase lifecycle

The forecast earns its way to acting, phase by phase.

<PhaseTrack
  label="The phases, in order"
  phases={[{ name: 'Observing', note: 'its first week' }, { name: 'DailySuggesting', note: 'scoring each day' }, { name: 'DailyActive', note: 'acts on the day' }, { name: 'WeeklySuggesting', note: 'scoring each week' }, { name: 'FullyActive', note: 'acts on both' }]}
/>

| Phase | Reached when | What it does |
|-------|--------------|--------------|
| `Observing` | No day of forecasts is scored yet | Collects data, its first week. An hour of the week is scored from the second time it's seen. No predictions yet |
| `DailySuggesting` | Every hour of the day is observed, and a day of forecasts is scored | Scores daily confidence, without acting on it |
| `DailyActive` | Daily confidence reaches the threshold | Predictions drive decisions. Weekly patterns are still learning |
| `WeeklySuggesting` | Every hour of the week is observed at least once | Earns weekly confidence |
| `FullyActive` | Weekly confidence reaches the threshold | Daily and weekly patterns both drive decisions |

| Case | What it means |
|---|---|
| The gates | They count hours of the calendar, not data points. A workload observed only from 9 to 5 hasn't shown its nights, however long it's watched |
| A workload paused every night with neither the [doorman](/concepts/wake-on-request/) nor a [Prometheus activity query](/guides/prometheus-signals/#while-the-workload-is-paused) | Its nights are never observed ([What an hour records](#what-an-hour-records)), so it never reaches `DailySuggesting`. Route it through the doorman for `autoResume` and the forecast's veto. That works with `request`, or with [`ask`](/concepts/wake-on-request/#asking-before-waking), whose paused hours there are learned from whether people woke it. A workload no Service routes requests to, such as a queue worker, needs an activity query that sees its demand. A query of its queue's length is one |
| The threshold | The [forecast setting](#forecast-setting)'s, and a change applies at the next reconcile. A phase is earned at the threshold and lost 5 points below it. So confidence hovering there doesn't switch the forecast on and off every hour |
| `status.prediction` | A phase per season. `dailyPhase` and `weeklyPhase` are each `Observing`, `Suggesting` or `Active`, next to `dailyConfidence` and `weeklyConfidence`. `DailyActive` is `dailyPhase: Active` with `weeklyPhase: Observing` |
| After a [regime change](#anomaly-detection) | The phase shown, there and in `napwake_prediction_phase`, is the one the forecast acts in. Left at `WeeklySuggesting` with nothing scored, it shows `DailySuggesting`, until a day of its forecasts is scored and it drives decisions again |

## Forecast setting

How soon the forecast acts is one setting, `eager`, `balanced` (the default) or `cautious`.

| Set it on | With |
|-----------|------|
| An [opted-in](/guides/opt-in/#settings) workload or its namespace | The annotation `napwake.dev/forecast` |
| Every opted-in workload | The Helm value `defaults.forecast` |
| A ManagedWorkload you write | `spec.prediction.mode` |
| A ManagedWorkload, with a threshold of your own | `spec.prediction.confidence`, from 50 to 100, which overrides the setting's. A label-created ManagedWorkload keeps it when its annotations are applied again |

```yaml
apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: preview-api
  annotations:
    napwake.dev/forecast: eager
```

| Setting | Acts at a confidence of | What it trades |
|---------|------------------------|----------------|
| `eager` | 60% | Acts on less regular patterns that `balanced` waits out. Wakes a workload for nothing more often |
| `balanced` | 75% | The default. Acts on a weekday pattern after 8 days. Almost never wakes a steadily used workload for nothing |
| `cautious` | 85% | Acts only on patterns it forecasts well. For workloads costly to keep awake for nothing, such as one on GPUs |
| A threshold below 60 | | Gains a morning or so on the most irregular patterns, for two more hours woken for nothing |
| A threshold above 85 | | Loses mornings about as fast as it saves hours |

A wrong forecast costs a little money, never availability. It only wakes a workload early or keeps it up longer. A paused workload still wakes on a request through the doorman, or with [`ask`](/concepts/wake-on-request/#asking-before-waking) on a person's click.

So the choice is between mornings ready and hours awake for nothing. Twenty simulated workloads averaged this over eight weeks of loosely kept office hours. People arrived up to an hour either side of 09:10, and were away one weekday in four.

| Setting | Mornings ready, of 30 | Hours woken for nothing |
|---------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| `eager` | 10 | 14 |
| `balanced` | 8 | 8 |
| `cautious` | 4 | 5 |

On steadily kept office hours, every setting acts after 8 days. Every morning after is ready, and none wakes the workload on a weekend. The settings differ only on patterns the forecast can't get right every day.

### How long it takes

Days to `DailyActive` / `FullyActive`, learning live from a fresh start. Each workload uses 40% of its CPU request in use and 1% otherwise. `autoResume` is on, behind the doorman.

The queue worker is woken by a Prometheus activity query instead. The public preview is woken only by people, with `wake: ask`. "Never" means not within ten weeks.

| Workload | `eager` (60) | `balanced` (75) | `cautious` (85) |
|----------|--------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Busy on weekdays from 09:10 to 17:30, quiet nights and weekends | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |
| The same, with people arriving and leaving up to 20 minutes either side and away one weekday in ten (the median of 20) | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |
| The same, with people arriving and leaving up to an hour either side and away one weekday in four (the median of 20) | 8 / 29 | 9 / never | 10 / never |
| The same hours every day, weekends included | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |
| Always busy | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |
| Always idle | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |
| A queue worker with no Service, which a query of its queue's length wakes, busy every day from 07:00 to 19:00, paused every night | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |
| A public preview only people wake, with `wake: ask`. Someone clicks **Wake it** at 09:05 on weekdays, and it's quiet nights and weekends | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 | 8 / 14 |

A test in the repo runs these, and fails if they drift from this table.

| Pattern | When it acts |
|---------|--------------|
| Any the forecast can get right | At every setting, once it has scored a day of its second week. The first week is learned, not scored |
| Office hours | On the Tuesday of the second week. The Monday before is the only morning after the first week that the first request wakes the workload. Every morning after is ready, Mondays included. Weekends stay asleep, since the first weekend taught the forecast they're quiet |
| One it can't get right every day, as in the third row | A higher threshold waits for more evidence |
| Always busy, or always idle | Acting changes nothing. One is never paused, and the other never woken or held awake by its forecast of no demand |
| Any, before the forecast acts | Idle detection and wake on request work from the first hour. The first request of the morning wakes the workload, or with `ask` the first click |
| Any, with Prometheus history | The forecast [learns from its history](#learning-from-history) first, so a few weeks of a steady pattern acts from the first day |

## When it acts

`status.prediction` says whether the forecast is acting. If it isn't, it says what it waits for, and when it's expected to start.

| Field | What it says |
|-------|--------------|
| `acting` | Whether the forecast drives decisions. It holds off pauses before hours it expects busy. With `autoResume`, it wakes the workload 15 minutes ahead of them. With [`pauseEarly`](#pausing-early), it brings pauses forward before hours it expects quiet |
| `threshold` | The confidence it acts at. That's the setting's, or `spec.prediction.confidence` |
| `reason` | One of the reasons below |
| `message` | The same in a sentence, with its confidence and threshold once it has them |
| `startsActing` | When it's expected to start acting |
| `history` | What it learned from Prometheus when Napwake began managing the workload ([Learning from history](#learning-from-history)) |

| `reason` | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| `Acting` | The forecast drives decisions |
| `Observing` | It hasn't scored a day of its forecasts yet, as in its first week, or hasn't seen every hour of the day |
| `EarningConfidence` | Its forecasts of the last day aren't yet right enough |
| `Relearning` | Demand broke from what it had learned, after a [regime change](#anomaly-detection) |
| `NotLearning` | The workload is paused with nothing to see its demand. It has no doorman, and no Prometheus activity query that can be evaluated. Its message says why requests don't wake it, and how to fix that. With `ask`, queries that can't be evaluated leave it `NotLearning` too, even behind the doorman |

| A message | Ends with |
|-----------|-----------|
| While the workload is paused | What it learns from then, `requests through the doorman`, `its Prometheus activity queries`, or both |
| While it's paused behind the doorman and asks before waking | `people waking it (a click on its asleep page, a wake request or an activity annotation)`, with `and its Prometheus activity queries` if it has them |
| Behind the doorman, while its queries can't be evaluated | `its Prometheus activity queries failing`. Behind the doorman, it learns whatever its queries do |

A message while learning, then one while not learning, which says why and how to fix it, with a link:

```text wrap
learning: it has seen 100 of the 168 hours of the week, and scores its forecast of an hour from the second time it sees it. It's expected to start acting around Tue 24 Mar 00:00 UTC, if the coming days repeat the last week
```

```text wrap
not learning while paused: no Service routes requests to it. A request can't wake it, so its demand can't be seen. It learns only the hours it's awake. To learn the hours it's paused too, give it a Service, or, for a workload no request reaches, such as a queue worker, a Prometheus activity query that sees its demand: https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/wake-on-request/#when-a-workload-isnt-routed
```

| `startsActing` | |
|----------------|---|
| How it's projected | A copy of the forecast runs on through the demand last seen in each hour of the week, until it would act. It has the same phase gates and confidence, and assumes the coming week looks like the last |
| Unseen hours | An unseen hour of the week takes the same hour on the nearest day it has. An hour of the day it has never seen is never assumed. So a workload paused nightly with nothing watching is never projected to act |
| How close it is | On office hours, within a day of when the forecast does act, from its second day. Within an hour once it has seen a weekend |
| When it's projected | Again every hour, as the forecast learns |
| When it's unset | While the forecast is acting. Also when the forecast isn't expected to act within four weeks, as for a pattern too irregular for its setting. The message then says so |

`kubectl get managedworkloads -o wide` shows the reason in a `Forecast` column.

## Pausing early

With `pauseEarly` on, a workload idle for 20 minutes is paused without waiting out `idleAfter`, when all of these hold:

| Condition | What it takes |
|-----------|---------------|
| The forecast is acting, and sure the hours ahead are quiet | Every hour until `idleAfter` would run out, and at least the hour under way and the next, is forecast below `cpuThreshold`. It's judged as the veto and `autoResume` judge, at the same threshold. An unseen hour of the week can't be called quiet. Nor can the hours after one far quieter than forecast, as on a holiday |
| A request would wake it | Its `wake.mode` is `Request`, the default. A workload that [asks before waking](/concepts/wake-on-request/#asking-before-waking) never pauses early, since a request doesn't wake it. The [doorman](/concepts/wake-on-request/) is on and has a Ready pod, and the last pause found nothing wrong with routing (`WakeOnRequest`). Every Service that selects the pods is one the doorman would route while paused, on every port outside `napwake.dev/doorman-ignore-ports`. None is headless or has a UDP or SCTP port. None keeps traffic on the caller's node (`internalTrafficPolicy: Local`), or has another workload's Ready pods behind it. Behind GKE container-native load balancing, it has the [forwarder](/concepts/wake-on-request/#gke-container-native-load-balancing-negs). At least one routes TCP, and their ports fit the doorman ports the workload and its namespace may hold |
| Nothing else holds it | These hold an early pause, as they hold any. An `active-until` hold, awake [dependents](/concepts/dependencies/), and the hour Napwake waits after a [GitOps tool](/guides/gitops/#what-napwake-does) undid its last pause |

A wrong forecast then costs a request a wake, a cold start, never its answer. With `ask`, a wrong guess would cost a person a click, so a workload that asks waits out `idleAfter`.

| Case | What happens |
|---|---|
| Anything above doesn't hold, or can't be read | The workload waits out `idleAfter` as usual, and the operator logs why |
| Services without a selector, and `ExternalName` Services | Not checked, since nothing says which pods they reach |
| [Dry-run](/guides/dry-run/) | An early pause is a would-be pause, measured like any other |
| The [forecast setting](#forecast-setting) | A separate setting. It says how sure the forecast must be to act at all, and this one whether, once sure, it brings pauses forward. So a `cautious` forecast can pause early too |
| The hours of an early pause | Behind the doorman, learned like any paused hour ([What an hour records](#what-an-hour-records)), so the forecast keeps learning |

It's off unless you set `napwake.dev/pause-early: "true"` on an [opted-in](/guides/opt-in/#settings) workload or its namespace. The Helm value `defaults.pauseEarly` sets it for every opted-in workload, and `spec.idlePolicy.pauseEarly: true` on a ManagedWorkload you write.

```yaml
apiVersion: apps/v1
kind: Deployment
metadata:
  name: preview-api
  annotations:
    napwake.dev/auto-resume: "true"
    napwake.dev/pause-early: "true"
```

An early pause has a `PausedEarly` event in place of `IdleDetected`, naming the hours it expects quiet. Until the workload wakes, `status.activity.forecastQuietUntil` holds when those hours end. [`kubectl napwake status`](/getting-started/kubectl-plugin/#see-what-napwake-is-doing) says so in NEXT.

The event comes first here, then the status line:

```text wrap
idle for 20 minutes, since its CPU use was last above the activity threshold, and the forecast expects the hours from 18:00 to 20:00 UTC to be quiet; pausing before idleAfter (1 hour) runs out
```

```text wrap
paused early: the forecast expected quiet until Fri 20:00 UTC; wakes on a request or activity
```

### What it saves and costs

The pause comes once the hour people leave in is over, since that hour is learned as busy. On office hours ending at 17:30, it comes at 18:00. The activity clock would have paused at 18:29, an `idleAfter` after the last busy minute.

The longer `idleAfter`, the more that saves. Anyone who comes in an hour forecast quiet, before `idleAfter` would have run out, pays a cold start.

The simulator measures both at `balanced`, for a workload at 40% of its CPU request in use. It counts the weeks after the forecast acts. For a holiday that's its week, and for a change of hours the two weeks after.

| Workload | Hours awake a week, without → with | Extra cold starts a week |
|----------|------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Busy on weekdays from 09:10 to 17:30, quiet nights and weekends | 48.7 → 46.3 | 0 |
| The same, with `idleAfter: 2h` | 53.7 → 46.3 | 0 |
| The same hours every day, weekends included | 68.1 → 64.8 | 0 |
| Office hours, people arriving and leaving up to 20 minutes either side and away one weekday in ten (the average of 20) | 45.2 → 43.3 | 0 |
| Office hours, people arriving and leaving up to an hour either side and away one weekday in four (the average of 20) | 38.2 → 37.8 | 0 |
| Office hours, the week of a holiday on a Wednesday | 39.8 → 37.8 | 0 |
| Office hours moving to 13:00 to 21:00, the two weeks after | 56.0 → 54.7 | 0 |
| Office hours, someone coming back for ten minutes 15 to 50 minutes after people left, one weekday in three (the average of 20) | 50.6 → 48.6 | 0.6 |
| Office hours, someone working in the evening too, one weekday in three, in three bursts with up to 25 minutes between them (the average of 20) | 55.4 → 52.2 | 0.5 |
| Always busy | 168.0 → 168.0 | 0 |
| Always idle | 0.0 → 0.0 | 0 |

A test in the repo runs these, and fails if they drift from this table.

| | What it means |
|---|---|
| The default `idleAfter` of an hour | Half an hour saved a weekday, about 5% of the hours awake. At two hours, an hour and a half a weekday |
| Learned patterns, holidays and changes of hours included | No cold starts. Nothing is brought forward on a holiday, since the forecast doubts itself from the holiday's first quiet hour. Nor on the first two days of new hours, while it relearns. And nobody comes back in the hours it pauses through |
| Loosely kept hours | They save less. There are fewer days people leave to bring a pause forward from, and fewer evenings the forecast is sure of |
| Cold starts | They come from use it can't foresee within `idleAfter` of the last. Someone comes back after the others left, or works in the evening in bursts. Each gap over 20 minutes, in an hour learned as quiet, finds the workload paused |
| The 20 minutes | At 10, evening work like that row's costs three times the cold starts, for a quarter more of the hours saved. On office hours, any wait up to half an hour saves the same |

Turn it on for workloads used to a pattern. There, an hour after the last use is an hour paid for nothing every day. Leave it off for one people come back to at odd hours, or set `active-until` while they do.

## Learning from history

With a Prometheus URL (`prometheus.url`, `--prometheus-url`), a new ManagedWorkload's forecast starts from the workload's history. The operator reads up to the last four weeks of its CPU. It learns them in one go, as it would have live.

Then it carries on live. A steady pattern acts from the first day. An irregular or short history goes on learning, through the same phase gates and confidence.

| History | How it's read |
|---|---|
| An hour of history | Measured as a live hour is. That's the workload's own containers' usage against their requests, sidecars injected into its pods left out, at its busiest moment. Above `cpuThreshold`, it counts as at least twice `cpuThreshold` |
| Its pods | Found by the names a Deployment or StatefulSet gives them, so old revisions' pods are part of its history too |
| A Prometheus holding more than one cluster's series, such as Thanos, Mimir or a shared one | Set `prometheus.selector` (`--prometheus-selector`), such as `cluster="staging"`. Its matchers are added to the query, so a same-named workload in another cluster isn't read as this one |
| The query | One range query per workload, a point an hour, of the busiest five-minute rate of `container_cpu_usage_seconds_total` in each hour. Prometheus works it out, and answers with at most 672 points |
| When it fails | The operator asks for one workload at a time, and waits up to 30 seconds. A failing query is tried twice more, two minutes apart, before the forecast learns live only |
| A reconcile | Never held up. The forecast learns live meanwhile, and takes up what was learned once it's ready |
| How often | Once per ManagedWorkload, when it's new. One that has learned live keeps what it learned. Deleting and creating it again starts it afresh, from history again |

| What history can't tell | What it assumes |
|-------------------------|-----------------|
| Hours none of its pods ran in | Quiet, as an hour scaled to zero by something else is live. That includes hours Napwake paused it, if it managed the workload before. One paused behind the doorman or watched by an activity query is quiet anyway. One paused with neither, which live learning skips, can't be told from an hour nobody used |
| Its CPU requests | They're today's. If they changed over the four weeks, earlier hours are measured against the new ones |
| Use seen only in requests or activity annotations | None, since that isn't in Prometheus. So a light-use workload's history looks quieter than its live hours will |
| Bursts | Five-minute rates are smoother than the minute-by-minute readings live learning takes. A burst of a minute or two counts for less |

`status.prediction.history` says what came of it, with an event once:

| `history.reason` | Event | Meaning |
|------------------|-------|---------|
| `Learned` | `ForecastLearnedHistory` | Learned `hours` of history, `from` and `to`. The message says how much, such as `the forecast learned from 4 weeks of the workload's CPU history in Prometheus` |
| `NoHistory` | `ForecastLearningLive` | Prometheus has no CPU for its pods, as for a workload deployed with it. The forecast learns from now |
| `NoCPURequests` | `ForecastHistoryUnavailable` (warning) | Its containers request no CPU, so there's nothing to measure its use against |
| `QueryFailed` | `ForecastHistoryUnavailable` (warning) | Prometheus didn't answer three times. The message has its error |
| `NoPrometheusURL` | none | The operator has no Prometheus URL, so the forecast learns live from the start |

[`kubectl napwake forecast`](/getting-started/kubectl-plugin/#replay-the-forecast) shows what the forecast would make of your workloads' history before you opt them in.

## What an hour records

The forecast learns CPU utilisation, as a percentage of what the workload's own containers request, in the pods reporting it. That's the measure `cpuThreshold` is set in ([Idle detection](/concepts/idle-detection/#what-counts-as-activity)).

The veto and the wake ahead compare the forecast with `cpuThreshold` directly. So a forecast that keeps an idle workload awake would also wake it once paused.

Each hour is fed in once it's over, as the busiest moment seen in it. It's recorded only if it was checked from its start to its end.

| While the workload is | The hour records |
|-----------------------|------------------|
| Awake | The highest utilisation, from CPU read every minute. The first 15 minutes after a wake aren't counted, whatever woke it, `kubectl scale` included. After that, an hour the [activity clock](/concepts/idle-detection/#what-counts-as-activity) sees it in use counts as at least twice `cpuThreshold`. That's use from CPU above `cpuThreshold`, an activity annotation or a Prometheus query |
| Pausing, and a request through the doorman calls the pause off, with `wake.mode: Request` | At least twice `cpuThreshold`, as a request that wakes it from zero. Its pods never stopped, so the first 15 minutes after the wake would hide it otherwise. A wake request, an activity annotation or, with `Ask`, a click that calls a pause off isn't counted itself. The hour records what the workload uses after those 15 minutes |
| Paused behind the [doorman](/concepts/wake-on-request/) (`WakeOnRequest=True`), with `wake.mode: Request` | No demand, while a check finds it still paused, since a request would have woken it. A request that wakes it counts as at least twice `cpuThreshold`, however little it then uses. So do a [wake request](/guides/pause/#wake-now) and an activity annotation. That holds while its Prometheus activity queries can't be evaluated. The hour is busy if a request woke it or a query found activity, and quiet otherwise |
| Paused behind the doorman, with [`wake.mode: Ask`](/concepts/wake-on-request/#asking-before-waking) | No demand, while a check finds it still paused, since someone who needed it would have woken it. A person's wake counts as at least twice `cpuThreshold`. That's a click on **Wake it**, a wake request or an activity annotation. So does a query finding activity |
| With `Ask`, while the doorman doesn't route it, or its queries can't be evaluated | Demand is seen only if someone woke it, so the hour isn't learned. Nobody can click then, and the doorman sees only people. So an outage of the doorman or of Prometheus can't teach it quiet hours |
| With `Ask`, and no Service to route, such as a queue worker | It learns from its queries while they can be evaluated, as with `Request` |
| Paused with a [Prometheus activity query](/guides/prometheus-signals/#while-the-workload-is-paused) | The query runs every minute, as while awake, and wakes the workload when it finds activity. A quiet check finds no demand, and activity counts as at least twice `cpuThreshold`. A query that can't be evaluated never wakes it. Without the doorman, that hour isn't recorded |
| Paused with neither watching, and `wake.mode: Request` | Nothing. Demand it can't see isn't zero |

A workload whose use stays under `cpuThreshold`, with no other source, is idle to Napwake while awake. So `autoResume` can't learn its mornings. Lower `cpuThreshold` below its use, or add a source that sees it.

<details class="nw-details">
<summary>Why an hour records what it does</summary>

| Rule | Why |
|------|-----|
| An hour records its peak | The activity clock counts any minute above `cpuThreshold` as activity. An hour busy only for its last ten minutes is an hour the workload has to be awake for |
| The first 15 minutes after a wake don't count | Starting up isn't demand. Counted, a wake ahead of a busy hour would teach the forecast that the hour before is busy too. It would wake the workload earlier every week |
| An hour in use counts as at least twice `cpuThreshold` | A light-use workload's use may show only in an annotation or a query. Without the floor, it would learn light use on mornings `autoResume` had it ready. On mornings people woke it, it would learn a request's twice `cpuThreshold`. The forecast would then have it ready only every other morning |
| A waking request counts as twice `cpuThreshold` | So the hour people arrive in is learned as busy. A wake request, an activity annotation and a click count the same |
| A request that calls a pause off counts as one that wakes it | Its pods never stopped, so the 15 minutes after the wake would hide it. A workload whose use shows only in requests, its CPU under `cpuThreshold`, would learn those hours from its CPU. The forecast would then let it be paused in them again |
| With `ask`, a paused hour behind the doorman nobody woke it in is quiet | Only people wake it then. Someone who needed it would have clicked or asked |
| Only where people could have woken it | So an outage can't teach the forecast quiet hours |
| A query watches a paused workload | That's how a workload no Service routes requests to, such as a queue worker, learns its nights. It uses a query of its queue's length |
| The hour under way is kept in memory | After an operator restart or a leader change, that hour is skipped, unless it was only minutes old. It isn't recorded from the part seen after |

</details>

## The model

Holt-Winters keeps four components, and moves each a small step towards every hour it sees. A component's smoothing parameter sets the size of its step.

| Component | What it represents | Smoothing parameter |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Level | The mean demand | $$\alpha = 0.01$$ |
| Trend | Whether demand is growing or shrinking | $$\beta = 0.001$$ |
| Daily components | 24 offsets, one per hour of day, the shape of an average day | $$\gamma_1 = 0.1$$ |
| Weekly components | 168 offsets, one per hour of week, how each weekday departs from that shape | $$\gamma_2 = 0.5$$ |

Each hour, the model compares the hour's utilisation $$Y(t)$$ with what it predicted, for confidence scoring. Then it updates all four by exponential smoothing.

It's additive, since idle workloads spend much of their time at zero demand. A multiplicative model, which divides by the level and the seasonal factors, diverges there.

The forecast $$h$$ hours ahead adds the level, $$h$$ hours of trend, and that hour's daily and weekly offsets. It's never negative:

$$
F(t+h) = \max\Bigl(0,\; L(t) + h \cdot T(t) + D(t+h) + W(t+h)\Bigr)
$$

<details class="nw-details">
<summary>The update equations</summary>

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(t) &= \max\Bigl(0,\; \alpha \cdot \bigl(Y(t) - D(t) - W(t)\bigr) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \bigl(L(t{-}k) + k \cdot T(t{-}k)\bigr)\Bigr) \\[6pt]
T(t) &= \beta \cdot \frac{L(t) - L(t{-}k)}{k} + (1 - \beta) \cdot T(t{-}k) \\[6pt]
D(t) &= \gamma_1 \cdot \bigl(Y(t) - L(t) - W(t)\bigr) + (1 - \gamma_1) \cdot D(t - s_1) \\[6pt]
W(t) &= \gamma_2 \cdot \bigl(Y(t) - L(t) - D(t)\bigr) + (1 - \gamma_2) \cdot W(t - s_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

$$s_1 = 24$$ is the daily season's length, and $$s_2 = 168$$ the weekly one's. $$k$$ is the hours since the previous observation, 1 unless observations were missed.

After each update, the components are renormalised. The daily components sum to zero. For each hour of the day, so do the weekly components across the seven days.

The shift moves into the component above. That leaves every forecast unchanged, and keeps the level the mean demand, so it's never negative.

</details>

| | What the model does |
|---|---|
| A gap in the observations, from the operator being down or a workload paused with neither the doorman nor an activity query watching | Skips it, since there's no honest value to fill it with. Slots are [wall-clock hours](#wall-clock-alignment), so skipping one doesn't shift what was learned |
| The trend across a gap | Carries it, across a gap of at most a day |
| The first week | Initialises the model, as the first season does in classical Holt-Winters. An hour of the week seen for the first time sets its weekly component, so its forecast is what was seen. The level, trend and daily components learn only from hours seen again |
| One week of office hours | Forecasts the next week as the week it saw, weekends quiet |
| An hour of the week seen for the first time | Doesn't score it, since it had nothing to forecast it from. The forecast doesn't act on an hour it hasn't seen |

## Confidence scoring

Confidence is one minus the forecast's error as a share of demand. It's $$1 - \text{WAPE}$$, the weighted absolute percentage error. That's the total absolute error of the hourly forecasts over a window, divided by the total demand in it.

$$
C = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i} |F(i) - Y(i)|}{\max\bigl(\sum_{i} Y(i),\; n \cdot f\bigr)}
$$

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A confidence of 75% | The total error over the window is 25% of the demand in it |
| Zero demand | Defined, unlike an average of per-hour percentage errors. An hour of zero demand forecast as zero isn't counted as a perfect hour |
| The floor, $$n \cdot f$$ | $$f$$ is the workload's mean hourly demand over about a week, and at least 1% of the CPU requested. So a quiet weekend day is judged against a typical day's demand. A workload idling at a sliver of its request isn't judged on its noise |
| Daily confidence | Scored over the last 24 scored hours |
| Weekly confidence | Scored over the last 168 scored hours, so a whole week of weekdays and weekend must be forecast well |
| A window not yet full | No score until it is |
| An hour of the week seen for the first time | Not scored, or judged an anomaly |

## Anomaly detection

Traffic patterns change, with a marketing campaign, a new feature or a change of season. Napwake detects these regime changes with z-score anomaly detection. An hour is an anomaly when its error is more than three standard deviations from the recent errors.

1. Each hour, the signed prediction error $$e(t) = Y(t) - F(t)$$ becomes a two-sided z-score. It uses an exponentially weighted mean $$\mu$$ and standard deviation $$\sigma$$ of recent errors, which remember about a week. A sudden surge and a sudden disappearance both count.

    $$
    z(t) = \frac{|e(t) - \mu|}{\max(\sigma, f)}
    $$

2. If $$z(t) > 3.0$$, the hour is an anomaly. The model learns from it only up to 3 standard deviations, so a single spike isn't learned as a pattern.

    But if the last error in its hour of the week was as far out, the same way, it isn't an anomaly. It's a weekly pattern, such as a Tuesday night batch job, and it's learned in full.

3. 3 or more anomalies within 24 observed hours are a regime change.

| After | The forecast does this |
|-------|------------------------|
| An anomaly, or any hour in the day after a regime change | It holds the change to that hour of the week until the same hour the next day. If that hour breaks the same way, the change lasted and is made. If it goes as forecast, the day was a one-off, such as a holiday, and the change is dropped. The level and daily components learn as usual. Learned at once, a weekday holiday had the same weekday a week later forecast quieter than it is |
| An hour far quieter than forecast | It doubts itself. It neither holds off a pause nor wakes the workload. That lasts until an hour goes as forecast, or a regime change has it relearn |
| An hour far busier than forecast | Nothing, since the workload is in use then anyway |
| A regime change | It drops at least one level below the phase it held before the anomalies began (below). If they already cost it that much, by lowering its confidence, it stays where they left it. It's never a second demotion, and never a promotion. It discards the scored errors, the anomaly statistics, and which hours of the week it has observed. Whatever its phase, it acts again only once a full day of its forecasts is scored. A `RegimeChange` warning event is emitted once |

| Before a regime change | After |
|------------------------|-------|
| `FullyActive` | `WeeklySuggesting` |
| `WeeklySuggesting` or `DailyActive` | `DailySuggesting` |
| `DailySuggesting` | `Observing` |

| A change | What happens |
|----------|--------------|
| A weekday holiday | `autoResume` wakes the workload. The first quiet office hour is an anomaly, so the forecast doubts the rest, and the activity clock pauses it. That's an hour and a quarter awake for nothing. Three quiet hours make a regime change. The forecast is off until a day is scored, about 30 hours, so the next morning's first request wakes the workload. The forecast is back on during that day. That day is busy, so the holiday isn't learned into the week. The same weekday a week later, and every morning from the second after the holiday, is ready |
| A Saturday people come in | The same. The Saturday after isn't woken, and the Monday after is the morning it costs |
| A lasting change of hours, such as 09:00 to 13:00 | A regime change within the day, learned into the week from its second day. For about a week people wake the workload themselves. At `balanced`, every afternoon from the second Tuesday is ready. Within two weeks it stops waking the workload for nothing. Until then it does a few times, for the old hours and, on weekends, the new ones |
| A workload nobody uses any more | Woken for its old hours twice, on its first quiet morning and the same weekday a week later. Then it's left paused |

Idle detection and wake on request carry on throughout.

A single deviant day looks like the start of a new pattern, until the next day shows which it was. So the forecast doesn't guess. It costs the next morning, and nothing after.

## Wall-clock alignment

Seasonal slots are keyed to wall-clock time, not a running counter. So Monday 9am always maps to the same slot, whatever the gaps.

| Case | What happens |
|---|---|
| The time zone | The operator's, `--timezone` or the Helm value `timezone`, UTC by default. Each workload records it in `status.prediction.timeZone`, and [`kubectl napwake status`](/getting-started/kubectl-plugin/#see-what-napwake-is-doing) shows it |
| Daylight saving | Business hours keep their slots when the clocks change. Set `--timezone` to its users' zone, and a workload in New York or London is up at 08:45 local time every weekday. That holds through the change |
| Left at UTC | The operator's clock is an hour off its users' after the change, so the forecast has to learn their mornings again. In the simulator, four mornings that week find the workload paused. It's held awake for nothing on others, until about two weeks on |
| A time zone whose hours start at half or quarter past the hour in UTC, such as India's | Hours are counted on the local clock, and so are the 15 minutes `autoResume` wakes ahead |
| A workload paused for 6 hours without the doorman | It keeps its alignment. The next observation goes into the right hour-of-day slot |
| An hour paused behind the doorman, asking before waking or not, or watched by an activity query | Recorded as no demand. That's how the model learns that nights and weekends are quiet |

## Persistence

The learned state lives in the ManagedWorkload's status, in `status.prediction.state`. It's gzipped JSON, base64-encoded, about 2.5 KB, written with each hourly observation. No external database, persistent volume or ConfigMap is needed, and each workload has its own independent engine.

| Case | What happens |
|---|---|
| What it holds | The model's components, the scored errors, the anomaly statistics, and the weekly changes held for the next day. Whether the forecast doubts the hour under way. The last hour observed, and the demand last seen in each hour of the week, which [`startsActing`](#when-it-acts) is projected from |
| Operator restarts and leader changes | The engine survives them. An hour observed before a restart isn't observed twice, and the hour under way at a restart is skipped. Restarted daily, at a different hour each day, the operator loses those hours and nothing else. The forecast acts within hours of when it would have |
| A last observed hour more than an hour ahead of the operator's clock | Not trusted, since a wrong clock recorded it. The next hour is observed as usual, rather than refused until real time catches up |
| State that can't be read, from an older version or a hand edit | Discarded with a `ForecastReset` warning event. The engine starts learning again |

## Tuning

The [forecast setting](#forecast-setting), or `spec.prediction.confidence` in its place, is the only thing to tune. The smoothing parameters are fixed, the same for every workload, and can't be configured.

| Parameter | Value | What a higher value would do |
|-----------|-------|------------------------------|
| $$\alpha$$ | 0.01 | Level reacts faster to demand changes |
| $$\beta$$ | 0.001 | Trend detection is more aggressive |
| $$\gamma_1$$ | 0.1 | Daily pattern adapts faster |
| $$\gamma_2$$ | 0.5 | Weekly pattern adapts faster |

Lower values give smoother, steadier forecasts, and higher ones a more reactive but noisier model. The values were chosen by simulating office-hours, always-on, idle and noisy workloads.

A weekly component updates once a week. So $$\gamma_2$$ is high enough for a lasting change in a weekday's hours to be half learned the week after. The first week sets each one outright ([The model](#the-model)).

## Proving it

A forecasting mistake would otherwise show only after weeks in a real cluster. Such mistakes are a wrong slot, a late wake or a false wake at the weekend.

The repo's simulator, `internal/forecast/sim`, runs the operator's own forecast engine and decision rules. It runs them through simulated time a minute at a time, as the operator looks at a ManagedWorkload.

It covers:

- the activity clock pausing
- the veto
- `autoResume` waking 15 minutes ahead
- the doorman or a query waking the workload
- the warm-up after a wake
- each hour's peak fed to the forecast
- restarts, through the state saved in status

A quarter of office hours runs in about 30 milliseconds on an M3 Max (`BenchmarkOfficeHoursQuarter`). A test runs six weeks of office hours through both the reconciler and the simulator. It finds the same wakes and pauses at the same minutes.

The tests run in CI on every change. Besides [How long it takes](#how-long-it-takes), they check these:

| Scenario | What the tests check |
|----------|----------------------|
| Office hours | Once learned, the workload is up at 08:45 every weekday, 25 minutes before people arrive at 09:10. It's paused an hour after they leave, and left paused at weekends. At every setting, every morning from the day the forecast acts is ready, Mondays included, and it's never still paused on one |
| Every day, always busy, always idle, light use seen only through an activity annotation | Each acts as above. The light-use workload is ready every morning |
| Loosely kept hours | Ready on most mornings once learned. Woken for nothing about twice a month, for an hour or two each time. The settings trade as [Forecast setting](#forecast-setting) says |
| A holiday, a Saturday at work, a permanent change of hours, a workload nobody uses any more, daylight saving in New York and London with and without `--timezone`, daily operator restarts | As described above |
| Pauses with neither the doorman nor an activity query | The forecast never acts |
| Pausing early | On office hours, every weekday's pause comes at 18:00. Mornings are as ready, and the same hours learned. Nothing is brought forward on a holiday or the first two days of new hours. Nobody finds the workload paused who wouldn't have. Someone back after 18:00 finds it paused. Without the doorman, nothing is brought forward. The reconciler's six weeks bring the same pauses forward at the same minutes. [What it saves and costs](#what-it-saves-and-costs) is measured there too |
| A public preview only people wake, with `wake: ask`, paused every night and woken by someone at 09:05 on weekdays | It acts after 8 days, as behind the doorman. From then, `autoResume` has it up at 08:45, before they come. One nobody wakes is learned as quiet, behind the doorman |
| A queue worker with no Service, woken by a query of its queue's length, paused every night | It acts after 8 days, as behind the doorman. From then, `autoResume` has it up before the morning's messages |
| Learning from history | Four weeks of office hours have the workload ready from the first morning. The [projection](#when-it-acts) made in the first week is within a day of when the forecast starts acting |

Run the simulator with `go run ./hack/forecastsim`. It runs every scenario at every setting, and prints what it measured. These flags change what it runs:

| Flag | What it does |
|------|--------------|
| `-scenario holiday -setting eager -days` | Shows one scenario day by day |
| `-pause-early` | Brings pauses forward, counting the hours saved and the uses that then found the workload paused |
| `-wake-on-request=false -wake-on-query` | Runs a scenario as a queue worker its activity query wakes |
| `-wake-on-ask` | Runs a scenario as a workload only people wake, as `wake: ask` does |
| `-history` | Replays a workload's recorded CPU, as a percentage of its requests. It takes a Prometheus range query's JSON, or CSV of `time,percent` rows, with steps at most seven minutes apart |

On your own cluster, [`kubectl napwake forecast`](/getting-started/kubectl-plugin/#replay-the-forecast) replays the forecast over each workload's Prometheus history. It says whether the forecast would act if opted in today.

For the last two weeks, it counts the mornings each workload would have been ready. It also counts how often one would have been woken for nothing.

Named, as `kubectl napwake forecast api -n orders`, it shows [one workload's forecast](/getting-started/kubectl-plugin/#one-workloads-forecast). For a workload Napwake manages, that's its own forecast, from its status and saved state, whatever history Prometheus has.

It shows the phase, the hours of the day and week seen, and the hours learned from history and live. It shows whether it's acting or when it should, why, and the hours of the next day it expects busy. Then comes the replay of its history, day by day, when there's enough of it.
