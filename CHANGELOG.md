## [0.2.0] - Unreleased

The relaunch. The 0.1.x releases were early experiments, and 0.2.0 is a fresh start: install it new rather than upgrading.

### Renamed

Hybernate is now Napwake. Every name it leaves in a cluster or on your machine changes. None is kept for compatibility:

|---|---|---|
| Product | Hybernate | Napwake |
| Repository | `okedeji/hybernate` | `napwake/napwake` |
| Go module | `github.com/okedeji/hybernate` | `github.com/napwake/napwake` |
| API group, labels, annotations | `hybernate.io/…`, `hybernate.io/v1alpha1` | `napwake.dev/…`, `napwake.dev/v1alpha1` |
| CRD | `managedworkloads.hybernate.io` | `managedworkloads.napwake.dev` |

| Image | `ghcr.io/okedeji/hybernate` | `ghcr.io/napwake/napwake` |

There's no upgrade path from Hybernate 0.1.x or a 0.2.0 release candidate. Uninstall Hybernate, then install Napwake and opt your workloads in again with the new labels and annotations. [Moving from Hybernate](https://docs.napwake.dev/operations/upgrading/#moving-to-napwake) has the one thing to watch for.

### Security

- The asleep page's **Wake it** button posts a token the doorman signed, an HMAC-SHA256 of the workload's namespace, name and UID, the pause it was shown during, and an expiry 10 minutes on, checked in constant time. Only a post of a fresh token from the page wakes the workload, from a browser's `User-Agent` that says the click came from the page: over HTTPS, where browsers send fetch metadata, with `Sec-Fetch-Site: same-origin`, and from Chrome, Edge or Firefox with `Sec-Fetch-User: ?1`; over plain HTTP, where they don't, with an `Origin` that's the page's address, its host and port, which the page's `Referrer-Policy: same-origin` lets browsers send. A token for another workload or pause, or an expired one, wakes nothing, nor does a click a browser says came from another site, a script's, or anything's but a browser's; a browser from before about 2020 that says neither gets the asleep page again, asking it to wake the workload another way. A robot that forges those headers on purpose, or drives a real browser, can still click, at most once a minute. The old key stops passing 11 minutes after a doorman pod switches to a new one, and a token passes only in the spelling it was signed in. The page runs no script, its `Content-Security-Policy` and `X-Frame-Options` keep other sites from framing it, and its `Referrer-Policy: same-origin` sends them nothing. The doorman's replicas share the key through a Secret the chart and the Kustomize manifests create empty, `<fullname>-doorman-wake-key`, which the first doorman pod fills; a Role lets the doorman `get` and `update` that one Secret by name, and nothing else of Secrets. The doorman's admission policy also lets it set `napwake.dev/turned-away`
- A dependency is only learned from a request that woke a workload when the doorman wrote its `napwake.dev/last-request-from`, by the field manager the API server records, and only for a sender in the woken workload's namespace or one it allows with `napwake.dev/dependents-from`, the only namespaces searched for it. A search by IPv6 address reads at most 2000 pods. Before, anyone who could annotate their own ManagedWorkload could have the operator write a learned dependency and an event into the ManagedWorkload of whatever pod had the address they named, in any namespace, and page through up to 10000 pods per wake
- A dependency in another namespace, declared in `dependsOn` or learned, is only held awake, woken and waited for when its namespace allows dependents from the dependent's, with the new `napwake.dev/dependents-from` namespace annotation (namespace names, or `"*"`). Otherwise the dependent reports `DependencyNotAllowed=True`, with the same message whether the dependency exists or not. Before, anyone who could write a ManagedWorkload in one namespace could keep any other namespace's workloads awake, and tell from `DependencyNotFound` what was in it
- The doorman's exposure is documented, with an example NetworkPolicy: anyone who can reach a doorman pod can reach every routed, paused workload on its Service ports, and the workload's own NetworkPolicy sees the doorman as the source. See [Security](https://docs.napwake.dev/reference/security/)
- The doorman only dials Pod-backed IP endpoints of EndpointSlices the EndpointSlice controller manages, never loopback, link-local, or cloud metadata addresses (AWS's `fd00:ec2::254`, Alibaba Cloud's `100.100.100.200`), and only an address the pod an endpoint names has, in the Service's namespace (it may `get` pods for this), so a forged EndpointSlice can't send it past a NetworkPolicy into another namespace; it caps held connections (2048 per pod, 1024 per source IP, 256 per workload, and 128 per source IP and workload, so one workload that never becomes Ready can't take every place) and connections it passes through (1024 per pod and 512 per source IP, holding a caller over the cap until a place frees), closes a passed-through connection idle for 10 minutes or open 5 minutes after its route started draining, and rate-limits wakes per source and overall, holding a caller over the wake limit, or showing it the waking-up page, while the wake is retried. The Helm value `doorman.networkPolicy.ingressFrom` restricts who can reach paused workloads through the doorman, such as only the ingress controller's namespace, and `doorman.networkPolicy.egress` (off by default) adds an egress NetworkPolicy allowing the doorman only the pod CIDRs and the API server
- One workload may hold at most 32 doorman ports and one namespace 256 (`doorman.portsPerWorkload`, `doorman.portsPerNamespace`; `--doorman-ports-per-workload`, `--doorman-ports-per-namespace`), so one tenant can't use up the 10,000 the cluster shares; a workload over either reports `WakeOnRequest=False`, reason `TooManyPorts`
- The operator can only `get` ConfigMaps, one at a time, and leader election only needs Leases and Events: the chart no longer grants ConfigMap writes or unused leader-election verbs
- The scan's HTML report is written readable only by you, and a reported address is only its scheme, host and port: credentials, paths and query strings are never shown
- Released Helm charts are signed with cosign, like the image, and every release publishes a signed `checksums.txt` (`checksums.txt.sigstore.json`) covering the plugin binaries, `install.yaml`, the chart, and an SBOM for each of the image's platforms, and GitHub build provenance for the image and those files (`gh attestation verify`). See [Security](https://docs.napwake.dev/reference/security/) for the commands
- Every pull request and release scans the Go code with govulncheck and the image with Trivy, failing on reachable Go vulnerabilities and on critical and high image vulnerabilities with a fix; each release attaches Trivy's report for each platform (`vulnerabilities-linux-amd64.txt`, `vulnerabilities-linux-arm64.txt`). A release is scanned before anything is published, and `main` and the released image are scanned again every day
- Builds use Go 1.26.10 and `golang.org/x/net` 0.61.0, fixing every reachable vulnerability govulncheck reports, among them those in `net/http`, `crypto/tls` and `html/template` that the doorman and metrics servers reach; CI built with Go 1.25.3. The base images are pinned by digest, every GitHub Action by commit, and Dependabot updates them weekly. Workflows get read-only permissions unless a job needs more
- A ValidatingAdmissionPolicy (`doorman.admissionPolicy.enabled`, on by default; in the Kustomize manifests too, following the namespace and name prefix of any overlay on them) lets the doorman's ServiceAccount change only the `napwake.dev/last-request`, `last-request-from` and `turned-away` annotations of ManagedWorkloads, though its role can patch them, and a second, `<fullname>-request-stamps`, lets no one else set them, not even under Napwake's field manager, which the operator also checks
- Kubernetes' own namespaces (`kube-*`) and Napwake's own are always protected unless labelled `napwake.dev/allow-protected: "true"`, and Napwake never manages its own operator or doorman, whatever its namespace allows (`Protected=True`, reason `NapwakeComponent`): labelling every namespace can't pause CoreDNS, metrics-server, or Napwake itself

### Added

- Asking before waking, for environments on a public URL that crawlers and scanners would keep awake, such as a preview environment for each pull request: with `spec.wake.mode: Ask` (`napwake.dev/wake: ask` with the label, or the Helm value `defaults.wake: ask`), the doorman routes the paused workload as before, but only a person wakes it. A browser opening a page, or posting a small form that isn't the button's, such as the app's login form, gets an asleep page, a `200` with `Cache-Control: no-store`, `X-Robots-Tag: noindex` and `Referrer-Policy: same-origin`, with a **Wake it** button; a click wakes the workload as a held request does, and the waking-up page follows. Everything else, crawlers, `curl`, `HEAD`, API clients and scanners, gets a `503` with `Retry-After: 300` and a short text or JSON body saying how to wake it, and a connection that isn't HTTP is closed; both are counted as turned away, and nothing wakes. Probes, health checks and hidden files are answered as before, and once it's waking, callers are answered as for `Request`. A click wakes a workload at most once a minute, and a workload that asks never pauses early, since someone who came early would find it asleep. `WakeOnRequest` says the doorman asks; `kubectl napwake status` says `wakes on a click, a wake request or activity`. See [Asking before waking](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/wake-on-request/#asking-before-waking)
- What a workload that asks turns away: `status.turnedAway` counts the requests the doorman turned away during its current or last pause, a count only, brought up to date about once a minute from the doorman's `napwake.dev/turned-away` annotation rather than written once a request; `kubectl napwake status` lists the workloads that turned requests away, and `napwake_doorman_wakes_total` gains the results `asked` and `turned_away`. Many mean something automated calls the workload
- Public preview environments, a guide for teams with an environment for each pull request on a public URL: opting in each preview's namespace with `napwake.dev/wake: ask` from a Namespace, an Argo CD Application's `managedNamespaceMetadata` or a kustomization, or every workload with `defaults.wake`; having what only other workloads call, such as an API or a database, woken with what people open, as its dependency, or on any request; what people and robots see; waking a preview from a GitHub Actions workflow on every push, with `kubectl napwake wake`, or with a portal's `PATCH`, and the access each takes, `list` included, which `kubectl wait` needs; what the forecast and `autoResume` learn from people's wakes, and how long that takes; and what's turned away. See [Public Preview Environments](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/preview-environments/)
- Forecast setting in plain words: `napwake.dev/forecast` (`eager`, `balanced` or `cautious`) on an opted-in workload or its namespace, the Helm value `defaults.forecast` (`--default-forecast`), and `spec.prediction.mode` on a ManagedWorkload (`balanced` by default) say how soon the forecast acts, at a confidence of 60, 75 or 85: eager acts sooner, and on less regular patterns, and wakes a workload for nothing more often; cautious acts later, for workloads costly to keep awake. `spec.prediction.confidence` overrides the setting's threshold, no longer defaults to 75, and is kept when a label-created ManagedWorkload's annotations are applied again, except a stored 75, the old default, so the setting applies to workloads opted in before it. The thresholds were chosen by simulation, and the forecasting page says what each trades. See [Forecast setting](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#forecast-setting)
- A new workload's forecast starts from its history: with `prometheus.url` (`--prometheus-url`), the operator reads up to four weeks of a new ManagedWorkload's CPU in one range query, measured as live (the busiest moment of each hour, its own containers against their requests), and learns it in one go before carrying on live, so a workload with a steady pattern acts from the first day. A controller of its own reads one workload at a time, within 30 seconds, tried three times two minutes apart, so a slow Prometheus never holds up a reconcile. `status.prediction.history` says what came of it (`Learned` with `hours`, `from` and `to`, `NoHistory`, `NoCPURequests`, `QueryFailed`, `NoPrometheusURL`), with a `ForecastLearnedHistory`, `ForecastLearningLive` or `ForecastHistoryUnavailable` event. See [Learning from history](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#learning-from-history)
- `status.prediction` says whether the forecast is acting and when it will: `acting`, `threshold`, `reason` (`Acting`, `Observing`, `EarningConfidence`, `Relearning`, `NotLearning`), `message`, and `startsActing`, projected by running a copy of the forecast on through the last week it saw. `kubectl get managedworkloads -o wide` shows the reason in a `Forecast` column. See [When it acts](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#when-it-acts)
- `kubectl napwake forecast [NAME]` replays the forecast over the cluster's own Prometheus history, read as `scan` reads it: for each workload, whether it would act opted in today, and over the last half of the history, from a forecast that learned the first, with `autoResume`, the mornings it would have had the workload ready, those it would have found it paused, the wakes for nothing and when it would have woken it. `--forecast`, `--timezone`, `--window` (up to 34 days), and scan's Prometheus flags; NAME shows one workload day by day; table, JSON or YAML. See [Replay the Forecast](https://docs.napwake.dev/getting-started/kubectl-plugin/#replay-the-forecast)
- A forecast simulator, `internal/forecast/sim`, runs the operator's own forecast engine and decision rules a minute at a time through months of synthetic or recorded traffic in milliseconds, and its tests run in CI: office hours, every day, always busy and idle, loosely kept and irregular hours, a holiday, a Saturday at work, a permanent change of hours, a workload nobody uses any more, daylight saving in New York and London, daily operator restarts, pauses without the doorman, light use, a warm start, and the projection of when a forecast acts. A test runs six weeks of office hours through the reconciler and the simulator and finds the same wakes and pauses at the same minutes, and the forecasting page's timings are asserted against it. `go run ./hack/forecastsim` prints what it measures for each setting, or replays a workload's CPU recorded from Prometheus. See [Proving it](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#proving-it)

- Wake on request behind GKE container-native load balancing (NEGs), an optional mode for teams reached through a GKE Ingress or Gateway, off by default: with `doorman.negForwarder.enabled` (`--neg-forwarder`), while a workload with a Service behind NEGs is paused, the operator runs a forwarder Deployment beside it, `napwake-forwarder-<workload>`, whose pods GKE's NEG controller accepts as the Service's endpoints, since they're in its namespace, and which pass every connection, untouched, to the doorman (`/manager --forwarder`). It runs two pods of 10m CPU and 32Mi (`doorman.negForwarder.replicas`, `doorman.negForwarder.resources`) spread across nodes, listens on the workload's target ports, which GKE's firewall rules already open to Google's load balancers, has no API access, meets the restricted Pod Security level, keeps mesh sidecars out, is Ready only while it reaches the doorman, and is deleted once the workload is awake and its route has drained, or with its ManagedWorkload. New `WakeOnRequest` reason `ForwarderNotReady`, saying why; `ForwarderStarted` and `ForwarderStopped` events; `status.doorman[].forwarded`. The chart adds a headless Service `<fullname>-doorman-pods`, a `<fullname>-forwarder` ClusterRole, or Role per watched namespace, granting `create` and `delete` on Deployments only in this mode, and admits forwarders to the doorman's NetworkPolicy. A request through a GKE Ingress whose Service is in NEGs, or through a GKE Gateway, wakes a paused workload only in this mode. See [GKE container-native load balancing](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/wake-on-request/#gke-container-native-load-balancing-negs)
- Pausing early, off unless you turn it on: with `napwake.dev/pause-early: "true"` on an opted-in workload or its namespace, the Helm value `defaults.pauseEarly` (`--default-pause-early`), or `spec.idlePolicy.pauseEarly` on a ManagedWorkload, a workload idle for 20 minutes is paused before `idleAfter` runs out when a confident forecast expects every hour until then, and at least the hour under way and the next, to be quiet, by the veto's own rules, and a request would wake it: the doorman would route every Service that selects its pods, on every port but those `napwake.dev/doorman-ignore-ports` lists, within the doorman ports it and its namespace may hold, so a headless Service or one with a UDP or SCTP port keeps it from pausing early, and the operator logs why. An `active-until` hold, awake dependents and the wait after a GitOps conflict still hold it, and in dry-run it's a would-be pause (`napwake_dryrun_actions_total{action="early_pause"}`). A `PausedEarly` event names the hours the forecast expects quiet, `status.activity.forecastQuietUntil` when they end, and `kubectl napwake status` says `paused early: the forecast expected quiet until Fri 20:00 UTC` in NEXT. In the simulator, on office hours at the default `idleAfter`, it saves half an hour a weekday (48.7 hours awake a week to 46.3, or 53.7 to 46.3 at `idleAfter: 2h`), with no extra cold starts on any learned pattern, holidays and changes of hours included; use the forecast can't foresee, such as someone coming back after everyone left one weekday in three, costs 0.6 cold starts a week. `go run ./hack/forecastsim -pause-early` measures it. See [Pausing early](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#pausing-early)
- `IdleVetoed` condition, reason `ForecastExpectsDemand`, while a confident forecast holds off a pause; its message names the hour the forecast expects demand, and an `IdleVetoed` event marks the start of each veto
- `status.cost` is per calendar month and priced on requests (see Changed): `tracked`, `lastAccumulatedAt`, `awakeCPUHours`, `awakeMemoryHours`, `storageHours`, `pausedCPUHours`, `pausedMemoryHours`, `savedCPUHours`, `savedMemoryHours`, `costThisMonth`, `freedThisMonth`, `savedThisMonth`, `savingsUnknown`, `costWithoutNapwakeThisMonth`, `projectedMonthlyCost`, `projectedMonthlyFreed`, `projectedMonthlySavings`, `running`, `pricedAt`, `pricedTemplateHash`. `kubectl get managedworkloads` gains `Saved` and `Freed` columns. `status.dryRun` gains `freedCPUHours` and `freedMemoryHours`, priced as `estimatedFreed`
- Freed and saved: Napwake reports what a pause freed apart from what it saved, and measures saved rather than assuming it. Freed (`freedThisMonth`, `projectedMonthlyFreed`, the `Freed` column) is what the paused replicas requested, priced at their nodes' list price; saved (`savedThisMonth`, `projectedMonthlySavings`, `savedCPUHours`, `savedMemoryHours`, the `Saved` column) is a lower bound on what came off the bill: every 5 minutes the leader sets what Napwake holds paused in each node pool (`karpenter.sh/nodepool`, `cloud.google.com/gke-nodepool`, `eks.amazonaws.com/nodegroup`, `kubernetes.azure.com/agentpool`) against the spare capacity of the pool's Ready nodes, and credits what wouldn't fit to the paused workloads there. With no autoscaler, or scale-down blocked, saved stays at zero while freed grows. A pause records its pools in `status.pause.resources.nodePools`. An operator limited by `watchNamespaces` can't see every pod, so it reports saved as `unknown`, with why in `status.cost.savingsUnknown`. New metrics by pool: `napwake_capacity_paused_cpu_cores`, `napwake_capacity_paused_memory_bytes`, `napwake_capacity_spare_cpu_cores`, `napwake_capacity_spare_memory_bytes`, `napwake_savings_realised_dollars_per_hour`. The operator caches pods for this, keeping only those bound to a node and not finished, as their node, phase, and requests (about 2.6 KB a pod), and nodes without their image lists; its role gains `watch` on pods. `kubectl napwake status` shows the two side by side (`FREED THIS MONTH` and `SAVED` columns, totals in the summary, JSON `freedThisMonth` and `savedThisMonth`), says why saved can't be measured where it can't, and, when less than half of what was freed was saved, says under Needs attention that nodes aren't scaling down (JSON `nodesNotScalingDown`), pointing at `kubectl napwake nodes`. `kubectl napwake scan` shows them for live workloads, per workload and in `totals`, and its estimates are of what pausing could free, never a saving it can't know before Napwake runs: `COULD FREE/MO`, "could be freed a month" in the report, and JSON `freeableBasis`. Dry-run measures only what pausing would free (`status.dryRun.estimatedFreed`). See [Cost Tracking](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/cost-tracking/#freed-and-saved), and [Node Pools for Real Savings](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/node-pools/) for turning freed into saved
- Node autoscaler detection: the operator looks every 10 minutes for what scales the cluster's nodes, Karpenter, Cluster Autoscaler, EKS Auto Mode or GKE Autopilot, logs what it finds when that changes, warning when nothing does, and exports `napwake_node_autoscaler{kind}`, `none` when it looked everywhere and found nothing and `unknown` when it wasn't allowed to look. `kubectl napwake status`, `scan` and `nodes` name it too (JSON `nodeAutoscaler`), and `scan` says what pausing frees comes off the bill only as nodes scale down. The operator's ClusterRole gains `list` on Karpenter NodePools. See [Cluster Autoscaler](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/cluster-autoscaler/#what-napwake-detects)
- `status.prediction.state`: the forecast's learned state, kept in the ManagedWorkload. State that can't be read is discarded with a `ForecastReset` warning event
- Operator flags `--max-concurrent-reconciles` (8) and `--timezone` (UTC; the forecast's hours and weekdays follow it through daylight saving)
- `--prometheus-selector`, and the Helm value `prometheus.selector`: label matchers, such as `cluster="staging"`, added to the history query the operator makes, for Thanos, Mimir or a Prometheus shared by several clusters, so a same-named workload of another cluster isn't learned as this one's. It takes matchers as `kubectl napwake scan --prometheus-selector` does; activity queries run as written. One that isn't label matchers, or one without `--prometheus-url`, stops the operator at startup, and the chart refuses the second. See [Learning from history](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#learning-from-history)
- Helm values `crds.install`, `maxConcurrentReconciles`, `timezone`, `logEncoder`, `metrics.readerSubjects` (bound to a new `<fullname>-metrics-reader` ClusterRole, so Prometheus can scrape secure metrics), `doorman.podDisruptionBudget`, `doorman.nodeSelector`, `doorman.tolerations`, `doorman.affinity`, `doorman.topologySpreadConstraints`, `nameOverride` and `fullnameOverride`; and a `values.schema.json` that rejects invalid values, and values the chart doesn't have, such as a misspelt `watchNamespace`, at install
- A doorman ServiceMonitor, a `NapwakeDoormanDown` alert, and `config/prometheus` for installs from the Kustomize manifests
- `napwake_build_info{version}`, from the operator and the doorman, which also log their version at startup
- `napwake_doorman_proxied_connections` and `napwake_doorman_port_conflicts`; `napwake_doorman_wakes_total` gains the results `canceled`, `ignored`, `limited` and `refused`
- Wake on request: a client that stays connected without sending anything for 3 seconds wakes the workload, for protocols where the server speaks first, such as MySQL; a connection closed without sending anything doesn't. HTTP `GET` and `HEAD` requests from probes, scrapers and uptime monitors (`kube-probe/`, `Prometheus/`, the blackbox exporter, `vm_promscrape`, `GrafanaAgent/`, `Alloy/`, `OpenTelemetry Collector`, `otelcol`, Datadog's agent and Synthetics, `ELB-HealthChecker/`, Route 53, Google Cloud uptime checks, `Envoy/HC`, UptimeRobot, Pingdom, New Relic, Uptime Kuma and Gatus, also when they follow `(compatible; ` in a browser-style `User-Agent`) get a `503` and don't wake it, until a pod is Ready, when they're passed through; other methods from them, such as Prometheus remote write, wake it. Google Cloud's load balancer health check (`GoogleHC/`) gets a `200` instead, without waking it: Google's load balancers send nothing to a backend whose check fails, so told the workload is down they'd drop the request that should wake it. A connection to a workload that already has a Ready pod is passed straight through without waiting for the caller to speak, and a held caller that sends a body and leaves is let go at once. HTTPS health checks on the traffic port can't be recognised and wake the workload; see the compatibility page. `doorman.healthCheckUserAgents` (`--doorman-health-check-user-agents`) adds your own checkers and scrapers. A request for a hidden file, such as `/.env` or `/.git/config`, gets a `404` and doesn't wake the workload, so scanners hunting for leaked secrets don't keep it awake. `napwake.dev/doorman-ignore-ports` on a Service keeps ports off the doorman. IPv6 and dual-stack Services are routed, one EndpointSlice per family. New `WakeOnRequest` reasons: `ServedByOtherPods`, `UnsupportedIPFamily`, `NodeLocalTraffic`, `RoutingFailed`, `DoormanDisabled`
- `kubectl napwake`: kubectl's connection flags on every command (`--kubeconfig`, `--context`, `--cluster`, `--user`, `--as`, `--token`, `--server`, `--request-timeout`, and the rest); `-A`/`--all-namespaces` on `status` and `scan`; a `version` command; `wake` and `deps` take the workload's name, `kind/name`, or the ManagedWorkload's name, and list the candidates when a name is ambiguous; `--timeout` on `status`, `deps` and `enable`
- `kubectl napwake scan` reaches any Prometheus: `--prometheus-selector` reads one cluster's series from a store that holds many, `--prometheus-header` (such as `X-Scope-OrgID` for Mimir), `--prometheus-bearer-token-file`, `--prometheus-ca-file` and `--prometheus-insecure-skip-verify`. A `--prometheus-url` that doesn't answer like a Prometheus API fails the scan
- `kubectl napwake scan` exits 1, after writing its report, when it couldn't read something your access allows; JSON and YAML gain `namespaces` and `incomplete`, and an empty cluster gives `"workloads": []`. A scan that runs out of `--timeout` reports the namespaces it scanned and lists the rest in `incomplete.unscanned`. Scanning every namespace, it lists each kind of object across the cluster at once, a page at a time, so a cluster of thousands of namespaces takes a few dozen requests

- Label opt-in: `napwake.dev/managed: "true"` on a Deployment or StatefulSet makes Napwake manage it, and on a Namespace, every Deployment and StatefulSet in it; `napwake.dev/ignore: "true"` leaves one out. Napwake creates a ManagedWorkload named after the workload, owned by it so it's deleted with it, marked `napwake.dev/from-label: "true"`, and without the workload's labels, so a GitOps tool's tracking label can't make the tool claim it. Settings are annotations on the workload or its namespace: `napwake.dev/dry-run`, `idle-after`, `cpu-threshold`, `auto-resume`, `depends-on`, `wake`, `wake-max-wait`, and `wake-page`. The workload's own wins, then its namespace's, then the cluster-wide defaults. Booleans are exactly `"true"` or `"false"`. A value that can't be read is reported in an `InvalidSetting` event and skipped, so the setting comes from the namespace, then the cluster default, then the built-in default; an unreadable `dry-run` turns dry-run on; a label value other than `"true"` is pointed out in a `LabelIgnored` event. The summary is dropped when dry-run ends. `kubectl napwake scan` shows it for each workload in dry-run, priced at the scan's prices, with a total in the headline, and its next steps give the `kubectl napwake enable` command
- `kubectl napwake enable [KIND/]NAME`: ends dry-run for an opted-in workload by setting its own `napwake.dev/dry-run` annotation to `"false"`, which wins over its namespace's and the cluster default, or `spec.dryRun: false` on a ManagedWorkload written by hand. `--all` does that for every workload in the namespace: when the namespace's annotation or the cluster default keeps any of them in dry-run, it sets the namespace's annotation to `"false"` and drops the workloads' own, or sets each workload's own when your access doesn't allow changing the namespace. - Waking-up page: a browser opening a paused workload is answered at once with a page that shows the address it opened and how long the workload has been starting, and nothing about the workload's names, and reloads every 3 seconds; the first reload after any of the workload's pods is Ready reaches the app. Only page loads get it (`GET` or `HEAD` with `Sec-Fetch-Mode: navigate`, or asking for HTML from older browsers); scripts, API clients, WebSockets, and other protocols are held as before, with the bytes read to tell them apart passed on unchanged. It's a `503` with `Cache-Control: no-store`. - `kubectl napwake wake NAME`: asks Napwake to wake a workload now, whatever its wake setting, with a fresh `napwake.dev/wake-requested` token on its ManagedWorkload (below). A paused workload wakes, with the workloads it depends on; a running one has its idle clock restarted. It waits for Napwake's answer, then until the workload is Running, and exits 1 with Napwake's reason when it won't wake it, or, when the wait runs out, with whether Napwake had taken the request up. `--for 2h` also keeps it awake for that long; `--wait=false` returns straight away. It refuses with the reason, before asking, when Napwake isn't managing the workload: a missing or ignored target, or another ManagedWorkload managing it
- `napwake.dev/wake-requested`: a token any tool sets on a ManagedWorkload to wake its workload, as `napwake.dev/pause-requested` pauses it, such as a pull request bot, a developer portal or a chat command waking a preview environment for someone. It wakes a paused workload whatever its `wake` setting, `ask` included, with a `WakeRequested` event, and restarts an awake one's idle clock, which in dry-run ends a would-be pause. One pausing is woken once the pause is done. Napwake acts on each token once, recording it in `status.lastWakeRequest`, and the `WakeRequest` condition says what came of it, in the past tense with the time, until the next request: `Woken` (`woken as requested at 2026-10-23 09:05 UTC`), `AlreadyAwake`, or `False` with why: `ScaledToZero`, `Protected`, `TargetNotFound`, `TargetIgnored`, `DuplicateTarget`, or `BeingDeleted`, answered before the ManagedWorkload goes. A token set before Napwake has taken the ManagedWorkload on, as by a tool that creates it and asks for its wake at once, is answered `AlreadyAwake`: it's taken on `Running`. One already there as Napwake takes up a pause whose record its status lost is answered `PredatesStatus` and not acted on, so a backup restored without status doesn't wake the workload again on a request answered long ago. A `pause-requested` token already on a ManagedWorkload as Napwake takes it on, or takes up such a pause, is answered `PredatesStatus` too, and never acted on. `kubectl napwake status` lists the wake. See [Wake Now](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/pause/#wake-now)
- The forecast learns from people's wakes with `wake: ask`. Only people wake such a workload while it's paused, with a click on the doorman's asleep page, a `napwake.dev/wake-requested` request or an activity annotation, so every hour it's paused behind the doorman is learned: busy if one of those woke it, or an activity query found activity, and quiet otherwise. A check while the doorman doesn't route its Services, or while its activity queries can't be evaluated, sees demand only if someone woke it, so an outage of the doorman or of Prometheus can't teach it quiet hours. Paused every night and woken by someone at 09:05 on weekdays, it acts after 8 days, as behind the doorman, and from then `autoResume` has it up at 08:45. With `wake: request` nothing changes. `status.prediction`'s message ends `while it's paused, it learns from people waking it (a click on its asleep page, a wake request or an activity annotation)`, and `kubectl napwake forecast` replays it so where the doorman would route it, `people` in WAKES ON and `forecast.wakes.people`. - `kubectl napwake pause NAME`: pauses a workload now, rather than when its idle clock runs out, such as a preview environment at the end of the day. It's an ordinary pause: the workload wakes on a request through the doorman, on activity, by `autoResume`, or with `kubectl napwake wake`. It waits until the workload is Paused, or Napwake says why it won't be (`--wait=false` returns straight away), and warns when a GitOps tool sets the replicas from Git and will likely undo the pause, with the one-time fix (see the GitOps check below). It takes NAME as `wake` does. Underneath, it sets the `napwake.dev/pause-requested` annotation to a fresh token, which any tool can set: an annotation, so Argo CD and Flux leave it alone on a ManagedWorkload kept in Git. Napwake acts on each token once, recording it in `status.lastPauseRequest`, and pauses by the rules of an idle pause: not while awake workloads depend on it, an `active-until` hold applies, its namespace is protected, or it's already at zero; under dry-run it counts a would-be pause. Asking overrides the hour Napwake waits after a GitOps conflict. When a confident forecast expects demand within the hour, Napwake declines the pause and says when, and `pause` asks whether to pause it anyway; `--yes`, or the `napwake.dev/pause-overrides-forecast: "true"` annotation with the request, pauses it without asking. The `PauseRequest` condition and an event say what came of each request. See [Pause now](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/pause/#pause-now)

- Wake on request: a request to a paused workload's Service wakes it. While a workload is paused, the operator adds an EndpointSlice to each Service that selects it, pointing at the doorman, a new Deployment (two replicas) that runs the operator image with `--doorman`. The doorman holds each TCP connection, wakes the workload through the `napwake.dev/last-request` annotation, and passes the connection to a Ready pod, or closes it after `wake.maxWait` (default 2m) with a `RequestNotServed` warning event. A workload woken this way records `lastActivitySource: request`. On by default for workloads Napwake pauses itself; keep the doorman off a port with `napwake.dev/doorman-ignore-ports` on the Service, or disable it cluster-wide with the Helm value `doorman.enabled: false`. Services in GKE container-native load balancing (NEGs) aren't routed unless the optional forwarder is on, with an `UnsupportedLoadBalancer` warning; see the compatibility page for the ingress controllers, Gateway API implementations, and load balancers checked. The `WakeOnRequest` condition reports whether a workload is routed; `napwake_doorman_wakes_total`, `napwake_doorman_wait_seconds`, `napwake_doorman_held_connections`, and the `NapwakeDoormanWakesFailing` alert cover it. The operator's ClusterRole gains read access to Services and full access to EndpointSlices
- Workload dependencies: `spec.dependsOn` lists the workloads a ManagedWorkload needs, in its own or another namespace. See [GitOps tools](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/gitops/#checking-the-fix-is-in)
- A pause made while a workload was in use makes Napwake wait longer before the next. A pause is wrong when the workload's own use undoes it within `idleAfter` or 15 minutes, whichever is shorter: a request, a person's click, a wake request or an activity annotation. Each wrong pause in a row doubles the idle wait, up to `idleAfter` plus an hour, and a pause that holds ends it. The wait only ever delays a pause, and `status.idleWait` keeps it through a restart. A pause someone asked for, the forecast, a Prometheus query and a waking dependent don't count. In the simulator, `orders/api` on the test cluster goes from 79 cold starts a working day to 10. See [Paused while in use](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/idle-detection/#paused-while-in-use)
- Napwake says what to change, with a number. A `PausedWhileInUse` warning event and condition say how soon the pause was undone and how long the clock waits now. When the CPU the workload used in use sits clearly above what it uses idle, it suggests a CPU threshold between them, such as `try napwake.dev/cpu-threshold: "0.4"`. Otherwise it suggests a Prometheus query that counts the workload's requests. Without a measure of idle use yet, it says so. `kubectl napwake status` lists it under Needs attention, and `napwake_wrong_pauses_total` counts wrong pauses by workload
- A CPU threshold can be a decimal, from 0.1 to 100: `cpuThreshold: "0.5"` as well as `cpuThreshold: 10`, the `napwake.dev/cpu-threshold` annotation, `defaults.cpuThreshold`, and the plugin's `scan` and `forecast` `--cpu-threshold`. The default stays 10. The scan shows CPU under 10% to a tenth, as `CPU 0.4% of its request`
- The simulator models how long a wake takes, and the back-off after a wrong pause, which the scan's replay uses too
- `kubectl napwake status` is in colour at a terminal. Paused is blue beside a shut door, resuming yellow beside one ajar, running green beside an open one. Needs attention is red. Set `NO_COLOR` for no colour. Piped or redirected output is plain text, as before

The cluster's name, and the scan's Prometheus source, are left out unless you tick the box. The page sends nothing anywhere
- `kubectl napwake status -o json` says what woke each workload. Its `wokenBy` is `request`, `activity`, `wakeRequest`, `forecast`, `outside` or `pauseCancelled`, from its latest wake, until it pauses again. A ManagedWorkload deleted and made again under the same name doesn't take on the old one's. Each recent event has a `kind`, `pause`, `wake` or `notServed`, and each wake its own `wokenBy`. Each problem names its workload's `target`, such as `deployment/worker`
- `kubectl napwake scan -o json` gives each workload's `idleAfter`: its own when Napwake manages it, otherwise `--idle-after`

### Changed

- `spec.wake.mode: Request | Ask` replaces `spec.wake.onRequest`, and `napwake.dev/wake: request | ask` replaces the `wake-on-request` annotation, with a cluster default, `defaults.wake` (`--default-wake`). A value of `napwake.dev/wake` that can't be read gives `ask`, with an `InvalidSetting` warning, so a typo never lets crawlers wake a workload meant to wake only for people. Waking can no longer be turned off for one workload, and the `WakeOnRequest` reason `TurnedOff` is gone: on a public URL, have the doorman ask; to keep the doorman off a port, use `napwake.dev/doorman-ignore-ports`; for the whole cluster, `doorman.enabled: false`. A ManagedWorkload's stored `wake.onRequest` is dropped once the new CRD is installed, and the old annotation is ignored, but applying a manifest that still sets it is refused, by `kubectl apply`'s default strict field validation (`unknown field "spec.wake.onRequest"`) and by a server-side apply, as a GitOps sync's can be (`field not declared in schema`): take it out of your manifests in Git as you upgrade, and put `mode: Ask` where it was `false`; see [Upgrading](https://docs.napwake.dev/operations/upgrading/#wake-on-request-from-before-020). The field is `mode`, not `on`, since YAML 1.1, which kubectl and Helm read manifests with, takes an unquoted `on` for `true`
- A Prometheus activity query now wakes a paused workload. This is new behaviour for every workload that has `idlePolicy.activity.prometheus` queries: they used to be run only while it was awake, to keep it awake. Now they're run every minute while it's paused too, and one that shows activity wakes it, with a `WokeByActivity` event, `a Prometheus activity query shows activity, waking`, and `lastActivitySource: prometheus`, and wakes its dependencies as any wake does. So a workload no Service routes requests to, such as a queue worker with a query of its queue's length, wakes when messages wait, rather than staying paused, its queue filling, until someone wakes it by hand. A query already showing activity as a requested pause begins, such as a queue with messages still on it, doesn't undo `kubectl napwake pause`: it wakes the workload only once it has been quiet and shows activity again, which `status.pause.queryActiveSincePause` records. A query that can't be evaluated never wakes it, and `PrometheusAvailable=False` says so, worded for a paused workload; behind the doorman, its paused hours are learned through requests meanwhile, as without queries. The forecast learns the hours a paused workload's queries watch, quiet while they find nothing, so a worker paused every night now learns whole days and acts after 8 days, as one behind the doorman does; before, it never acted. See [While the workload is paused](https://docs.napwake.dev/guides/prometheus-signals/#while-the-workload-is-paused)
- A forecast that can't learn while its workload is paused says why, and how to fix it: `status.prediction.message`, and `kubectl napwake status` FORECAST and `forecast`, give the cause from the `WakeOnRequest` condition's reason, such as `not learning while paused: no Service routes requests to it`, then the fix and a link to the section of the wake-on-request page that explains it, in place of `not learning: paused without wake on request`. While a paused workload is learning, its message says from what: requests through the doorman, its Prometheus activity queries, or both
- The forecasting page opens with what the forecast does: it learns for every managed workload whatever `autoResume` says, and holds off pauses on its own; with `autoResume` it also wakes the workload 15 minutes ahead of demand, and turning that on later loses nothing
- Napwake only pauses what's running, and only starts what it stopped: a workload scaled to zero by someone else (a person, a pipeline, or KEDA) is left at zero. It isn't paused, woken by a request or activity, or routed to the doorman, and reports the new `ScaledToZero` condition. A pause recorded at zero replicas is handed back at zero, and hand-back events say how many replicas the workload was left at
- `status.cost` covers the current calendar month in UTC, and everything is priced on what the workload's pods request, sidecars included, not on what they use. Rates come from `costTracking.rates`, then the list price of the workload's nodes, then the defaults, and are applied when the figures are worked out, so a rate change reprices the month. The hours are kept to a billionth of an hour; time is settled at every phase change, and a gap of more than 2 hours counts as 2. Projections are `pending` until a day of the month has been tracked. Freed becomes money only once nodes go, which saved measures
- The forecast is an additive double-seasonal Holt-Winters model, stable at zero demand, that learns each hour's peak CPU as a share of the app containers' requests, so the veto, `autoResume` and what a wake teaches compare the same thing whatever sidecars are injected. Confidence is 1 − WAPE, daily over the last 24 scored hours and weekly over 168. A phase needs every hour of the day, or of the week, observed, and is lost 5 points below the threshold; a regime change demotes it one level below where it was before the anomalies began, never above where it is, and discards its evidence, and the forecast doesn't act again until a full day has been scored. A large spike that recurs in the same hour of the week is learned as a pattern rather than clipped as an outlier. Missed hours are skipped, not filled in. `prediction.confidence`, when set, must be at least 50
- `spec.target` can't be changed once set, since a paused target would be left at zero
- `idlePolicy.idleAfter` and `wake.maxWait` take only durations Go can read, such as `90m` or `2h`; `1d` is rejected, where it used to be stored and then break every read of ManagedWorkloads in the cluster. `wake.maxWait` is at most 1h. `spec.prediction` can be left out
- CPU activity is measured against the requests of a running pod of the current revision, for the app containers, so requests a LimitRange or VPA sets count, a template that sets only limits works, and evicted or older pods don't skew it. A workload scaled to zero is idle, not unmeasurable. The forecast reports a phase that acts only while it acts, so status doesn't say `Active` while a regime change has it holding back, and an hour in which the workload was seen in use counts as busy, so `autoResume` wakes a lightly used workload every morning, not every other one

### Fixed

- The scan report keeps an idle workload's light on in the With Napwake picture when Napwake never pauses it: a StatefulSet that deletes its PVCs, a protected namespace, part of Napwake, or a ManagedWorkload without an `idlePolicy`. Before, only such a StatefulSet kept it, and only when its line led with the reason
- The status page's meter shows what came off the bill as the exact share of what was freed. A freed figure of `<$1` draws no bar
- The scan report and the status page read what they show from the data, never from the product's sentences, so a reworded line can't change them
- In dry-run, an operator restart no longer ends every would-be pause with `activity resumed (unobserved)`. A gap in watching doesn't wake a paused workload, so it no longer ends the would-be pause that stands for one either; activity seen after the gap still does. Before, every upgrade, and on spot nodes every preemption, split each would-be pause in two and added an hour awake, so dry-run understated what pausing would free
- An operator restart never brings a pause forward. It restarted an awake workload's idle clock only when what's written was over 2 minutes old, which nearly every restart found, and otherwise went on from what's written, which can be missing up to a tenth of `idleAfter`, at most 5 minutes, of activity the operator saw since, so the workload could pause that much early. A restart now always restarts an awake workload's clock, with an `Unobserved` event: it can delay a pause by up to `idleAfter`, as before, but never bring one forward. A paused workload and a dry-run would-be pause go on through it
- A gap in watching is no longer called activity. When one restarts a clock, an `Unobserved` event says so and for how long: `Napwake wasn't watching for 9 minutes, so its idle clock starts again`, or `couldn't read its activity` while a source was out, and after a restart `the operator restarted, or another took over, so its idle clock starts again; it wasn't watching for up to 4 minutes`. It comes with the first check that can read every source, so a restart whose first check can't is still announced. `kubectl napwake status` shows `unobserved until 3m ago` in LAST ACTIVITY, the scan `not idle yet: Napwake wasn't watching until 3m ago`, and `IdleDetected` says the workload was idle `since Napwake could watch it again after a gap`. Likewise the start of managing a workload, and its waking, which start the clock but aren't activity: LAST ACTIVITY shows `none since Napwake began managing it 3m ago` or `none since it woke 3m ago`, not `created, 3m ago`, and the scan `not idle yet: Napwake began managing it 3m ago` or `not idle yet: it woke 3m ago`, not `active 3m ago (created)`
- `kubectl napwake status` shows a would-be pause that has freed less than a cent as `<$1`, in WOULD FREE and the dry-run summary, not `$0`, which it keeps for a workload still waiting for its first: the operator's estimate is to the cent
- A dependency whose own activity restarts its clock no longer goes on saying it's kept awake for its dependents: `HeldByDependents` turns `False`, reason `NotIdle`, and `status` shows when it pauses rather than a hold from before
- `kubectl napwake forecast` replays each workload with what would wake it once it's paused, rather than as if the doorman routed every workload: a request through the doorman when one of its Services can be routed, its Prometheus activity query when it has one, both, or neither, when people wake it by hand and the forecast doesn't learn the hours it's paused. Whether the doorman is on, and its NEG forwarder, are read from the flags of the operator that manages the workload, so with `doorman.enabled: false` no request wakes it, and with the forwarder on its Services behind GKE NEGs do. The table says which in a new WAKES ON column, a named workload's replay in a sentence, and the JSON in `forecast.wakes`, with why a request wouldn't wake it
- In dry-run, a pause asked for while a Prometheus activity query shows activity holds the query back as a real pause would: the query ends the would-be pause only once a check has found it quiet and it shows activity again, rather than at the next check. Activity the still-running workload makes itself, such as the CPU of draining its queue, still ends the would-be pause, which a real pause, with nothing draining the queue, wouldn't. `status.dryRun.queryActiveSincePause` records it
- The forecast learns from live hours again. It had learned nothing since a workload's history was read, or since an operator restart: data points never rose, no `PredictionFed` event appeared, and no forecast could ever act. The operator worked out the start of the hour from the system clock, and Go compares two such times by the clock reading it keeps for measuring durations, which differs by nanoseconds from one look to the next. So each look seemed to start a new hour, and no hour was ever seen whole. Tests and the simulator use a clock without that reading, which hid it; the forecast's tests now read the clock as the operator does
- Dry-run measures a whole app's would-be pauses: a dependent in dry-run that would have paused no longer holds its dependencies awake when they're in dry-run too, so each of them goes on to its own would-be pause, as it would live. Before, a frontend idle for hours held every service behind it "awake", and dry-run counted only the frontend. A dependency that pauses for real stays held by a dry-run dependent, whose pods are still up. When the dependent's would-be pause ends, its dry-run dependencies are stamped with activity, as its wake would have woken them, so theirs end too and their clocks restart
- On GKE, a paused workload's Services are routed to the doorman, so requests wake it: GKE annotates every Service of a VPC-native cluster `cloud.google.com/neg: '{"ingress": true}'`, and every one was refused as `UnsupportedLoadBalancer`. That value only asks for container-native load balancing should a GKE Ingress use the Service. A Service is now left off the doorman only when GKE's NEG controller reports NEGs of its pods (`cloud.google.com/neg-status`), it asks for standalone NEGs (`exposed_ports`), or an Ingress GKE serves (`kubernetes.io/ingress.class` `gce`, `gce-internal`, `gce-regional-external` or `gce-multi-cluster`, or no class at all) uses it, and it's taken off or put back as soon as one of those changes. The operator's role gains `get`, `list` and `watch` on Ingresses
- A wake or a rollout no longer warns `NoPodMetrics`, telling you to check metrics-server, while the new pods simply have no metrics yet: `MetricsAvailable` is `Unknown`, with no event, while none of the target's pods is Ready (`NoReadyPods`) and for 3 minutes after one is (`AwaitingPodMetrics`). Only Ready pods that go on without metrics are `NoPodMetrics`, with the warning, which now gives the `kubectl top pods` command to check
- The forecasting page's How Long It Takes table, now measured by the simulator and checked against it in CI, was wrong for a pattern repeated every day, weekends included: the forecast acts after about 8 days, not 3. The page also says now what leaving `--timezone` at UTC costs when the clocks change
- A new forecast has the workload ready on Mondays once it acts: it learned each hour of the week half at a time, so for weeks it predicted weekday demand on Saturdays, lost its confidence every weekend, and the first request woke an office-hours workload on the Mondays of its second, third and fourth weeks. The first week now sets what the forecast expects of each hour of the week, as the first season does in classical Holt-Winters, and isn't scored, since there was nothing to forecast it from; an hour of the week never seen isn't acted on. Every pattern the forecast can get right now acts after 8 days at every setting (`cautious` waited 15 on office hours) and is fully active after 14, and no setting wakes a workload on its first weekends. `status.prediction.reason` is `Observing` through the first week, and its message counts the hours of the week seen. See [How Long It Takes](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#how-long-it-takes)
- A weekday holiday no longer costs a second cold morning a week later: its quiet hours were learned straight into that weekday, so the forecast lost its confidence on the same weekday the week after. A break from the learned pattern, an anomaly or any hour of the day after a regime change, is now learned into the week only once the same hour the next day breaks the same way, so a lasting change, new hours or a retired workload, is still learned within a week, and one Saturday at work no longer has the forecast wake the workload the Saturday after. The hour after one far quieter than forecast, the forecast neither holds off a pause nor wakes the workload, so a holiday costs an hour and a quarter awake instead of two to three, and loosely kept hours' absent days about half as long. Forecast state is version 5: state of an earlier version is reset with a `ForecastReset` event. See [Anomaly detection](https://docs.napwake.dev/concepts/forecasting/#anomaly-detection)
- `kubectl napwake scan` counts CPU as activity above `--cpu-threshold`, as the operator's activity clock does, rather than at it, in its history replay and in its judgement of current CPU, which also compares the share before rounding it to a whole percent. One rule, shared by the operator, the forecast, the simulator and the scan, decides it
- `kubectl napwake scan` allows for the longer wait after pauses made in use. A managed workload is idle only once its clock's `pauseAt` comes. So one Napwake now waits 20 minutes for, not its `idleAfter` of 5, is active until then, and BECAUSE says why: `after pauses made in use it waits 20 minutes, not 5 minutes`. Before, it was idle once `idleAfter` ran out, which the report calls when Napwake would pause it, and it went dark in the With Napwake picture. The history replay waits longer too, after CPU soon after a pause, as Napwake does. So use that keeps coming back soon after each pause no longer counts a pause and a wake every `idleAfter`, which overstated WAKES and what pausing could free
- The chart refuses a `defaults.cpuThreshold` the operator won't start with, so the install fails rather than the operator. Before, a string had no range, so `--set defaults.cpuThreshold=0.05`, `"0"` and `"150"` installed, and a number had no limit on decimal places, so `0.125` did too. Each left the operator crash-looping. A threshold is now 0.1 to 100, to at most two places, written either way
- A new workload's history is read again two minutes after a failed attempt, as documented, rather than on each of the updates a new workload gets in its first minute, which used up all three attempts in seconds and recorded a briefly unreachable Prometheus as `QueryFailed` for good. A failure to read the target or its CPU requests from the API server is retried rather than recorded as `NoCPURequests`, with its warning, and isn't counted against Prometheus; the activity clock reports it as `MetricsUnavailable`, with the error, rather than `NoCPURequests`

- In dry-run, an activity annotation stamped in the same second a workload went Idle ends the would-be pause, rather than being taken as older than it: annotations are stamped to the second
- A StatefulSet whose `persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy.whenScaled` is `Delete` is never paused, since scaling it to zero has its controller delete every claim: it reports `Protected=True`, reason `PVCsDeletedOnScaleDown`, with a `Protected` warning event, and one paused before its policy changed is handed back. Set `whenScaled: Retain` to let Napwake pause it
- Handing back a paused workload a KEDA ScaledObject scales, on deletion, the ignore label, protection, or a scale-up outside Napwake, holds KEDA at the restored replicas while scaling it up, so a ScaledObject that may scale to zero can't take it straight back down before it's released
- A paused workload whose ManagedWorkload loses its status, to a backup restored without status or the ManagedWorkload being created again, is no longer left at zero for good, with KEDA holding it there: before scaling a workload to zero, Napwake copies what the pause changed to its `napwake.dev/paused-from` annotation, removed when it's handed back, and takes the pause up again from it (`PauseRecovered` event). The operator's role gains `patch` on Deployments and StatefulSets for it. A workload left `Pausing`, `Paused` or `Resuming` without a pause record is no longer resumed from its live replicas and KEDA value, which a pause had already set to zero; nothing was scaled, so it's `Running` again (`PauseRecordLost` warning event)
- A workload is `Paused` once its pods have stopped, not as soon as it's scaled to zero, so it no longer shows `Paused` while a StatefulSet's pods are still running. Pods that don't stop are waited for as long as their termination grace period and a minute more, one after another for a StatefulSet; then it's `Paused` with `PauseIncomplete=True` and a `PauseIncomplete` warning event, until they've gone. `status.pause.pausedAt` is written when it's scaled to zero
- Someone scaling up a workload Napwake has scaled to zero while it's still `Pausing` wakes it, as for a `Paused` one, instead of the pause scaling it back down; and someone scaling a `Resuming` workload back to zero after the resume scaled it up (`status.pause.resumedAt`) leaves it off, with `ScaledToZero`, instead of the resume scaling it up again
- `GitOpsConflict` is no longer cleared as resolved by a wake after a pause of a few minutes, which a GitOps tool syncing less often hadn't yet had the chance to undo: like a pause that stays, one Napwake wakes must have held for the hour
- Deleting the ManagedWorkload of a paused workload hands it back once: while its doorman ports drain, it's no longer scaled up again on every reconcile, over someone who had scaled it to zero meanwhile, nor are its ScaledObject and dependencies touched again, and the `Resumed` event is no longer lost when there are ports to drain
- A ManagedWorkload that becomes the `DuplicateTarget` while it holds its workload paused hands it back, with its doorman routes, instead of leaving it at zero with nothing to wake it
- A ManagedWorkload deleted without its finalizer running, or deleted and created again under the same name, no longer leaves its forecast engine, activity clock, routing backoff and doorman port claims in the operator's memory
- A condition message is cut to the 32KiB the API server allows, and an event's note to 1KiB, ending in `…`, so one that lists many workloads can't fail every status write, or have its event dropped
- The CRD rejects an `idlePolicy.idleAfter` under `1m`, which paused a workload about as soon as each wake finished, negative `costTracking.rates`, more than 10 `idlePolicy.activity.prometheus` queries, and more than 20 `dependsOn`. The `napwake.dev/idle-after` annotation and `--default-idle-after` (`defaults.idleAfter`) take at least `1m` too, and a `napwake.dev/depends-on` listing more than 20 uses the first 20, with an `InvalidSetting` event
- A change to a Deployment, a StatefulSet or a ManagedWorkload no longer lists and copies every ManagedWorkload in the cluster to find the ones it concerns: they're found through indexes on their target and dependencies, a ManagedWorkload's own status writes, such as cost and activity flushes, no longer re-queue its related workloads unless its phase, spec or dependencies changed, and one dropped from `dependsOn` re-checks the hold it took part in
- Reconciling a workload no longer lists and copies every ManagedWorkload in the cluster, several times over, to decide whether it's held awake, which dependencies to wake and wait for, and whether another ManagedWorkload manages its target: the operator keeps the dependency graph up to date from its watch, and finds a duplicate through the target index. A deleted workload's doorman slices are listed from the API server when they're removed, not again on each reconcile while its ports drain. With 5000 ManagedWorkloads, deciding a hold went from about 5ms and 17MB allocated to under 2µs and about 1KB
- A wake is no longer queued behind the periodic checks of every awake workload: reconciles are queued by priority, with a request the doorman holds, an activity or pause annotation, a scale-up from zero and a resume under way first, and periodic checks last. `--max-concurrent-reconciles` (`maxConcurrentReconciles`) defaults to 8, since most of a reconcile is spent waiting on metrics and Prometheus
- A resume of a workload that wasn't wholly Ready before its pause, such as one with a pod crash-looping, finishes instead of staying in `Resuming` with KEDA held; readiness is judged only from a status the workload's controller wrote for the scale-up
- A dependency learned from a single request no longer ties two workloads together for good: one learned from a wake is forgotten after 14 days with no request confirming it (`lastSeen`)
- A namespace-level `napwake.dev/depends-on` no longer makes the dependency depend on itself, which held it awake as a cycle; `napwake.dev/managed: "false"` on a workload opts it out without a warning
- A doorman port stays draining across an operator restart or leader change (`status.doormanDraining`), so it's never given to another workload while clients may still send the first one's traffic to it; a deleted ManagedWorkload stays until its ports have drained, at most a minute
- Wake on request: removing `napwake.dev/last-request` from a paused ManagedWorkload no longer stops requests waking it; a browser sending more than 16KiB of headers, such as SSO cookies, gets the waking-up page; a request whose lines end in a bare LF is read as HTTP, so the probe and hidden-file rules apply to it; a Service with `internalTrafficPolicy: Local` is left off the doorman (`NodeLocalTraffic`), since most clients couldn't reach it; and an orphaning delete right after routing no longer leaves a slice behind
- The doorman drops a deleted workload's wake series, and the operator a ManagedWorkload's series when its finalizer was removed by hand. `napwake_idle_seconds` keeps counting while a workload is paused
- `napwake_workload_phase` is 1 for a workload's current phase only. Right after the operator wrote a phase, a reconcile could read the one before from its cache and set it back to 1, where it stayed. So a workload that had paused or woken was still `Pausing` or `Resuming` too, and `NapwakeWorkloadStuck` fired for nearly every workload once the chart's PrometheusRule was on
- An install that stops managing a ManagedWorkload, because another install's `napwake.dev/operator` annotation is now on it, drops the workload's metric series and what it kept in memory, as for a deleted one: its forecast, activity clock, routing backoff and doorman ports. A `Pausing` series left behind would have kept `NapwakeWorkloadStuck` firing
- Dry-run counts a would-be pause that ends because the workload was scaled to zero, ignored, or protected, and no longer panics for a workload whose activity was never measured
- A pause's resource snapshot falls back to the last one when pods can't be read, instead of recording that it freed nothing; the forecast keeps learning after an hour recorded by a clock that ran fast; and its state is encoded only when it changes
- The ServiceMonitors keep the metrics' own `namespace` label (`honorLabels`), so alerts and per-namespace queries see the workload's namespace, not Napwake's
- `kubectl napwake enable` refuses, and says why, for a workload Napwake won't manage: in a protected namespace, opted out, not yet given a ManagedWorkload, or with the operator not installed; asks for the kind when a Deployment and a StatefulSet share a name; and `pause`'s forecast question no longer counts against `--timeout`. `wake --for` keeps a longer hold already set; `deps` shows a dependency it can't read as unknown; `status` fails for a namespace that doesn't exist and keeps this month's freed and saved for a workload back in dry-run
- `kubectl napwake scan` scans a namespace whose ReplicaSets it can't read, counts pods of Deployments' deleted ReplicaSets in their history, resolves dependencies in time on large clusters, leaves last month's freed and saved out until the operator rolls the month, tells a 403 from Prometheus behind the service proxy from the proxy's own, and rejects a `--window` over a year and negative prices
- `make release` tags a release whose versions are already bumped, dates its changelog section, and refuses a `VERSION` that isn't a version; a release publishes nothing users install until its GitHub release is drafted with every file, so a release that fails can be run again rather than left half published; and the docs are published after each release from `main`, but not after a pre-release, which rehearses a release without moving `latest`

- `kubectl napwake scan` keeps Napwake's own operator and doorman out of an allowed namespace's apps, counts infrastructure in namespaces opted in with the label and keeps its dependencies, matches a pod-template-hash by the characters Kubernetes writes it in, and no longer blames Prometheus when reading the API server used up history's time
- Learning a dependency from a wake can't hold up the wake: it gives up after 2 seconds
- A pause is recorded before the workload is scaled, so a pause interrupted at any point, by a crash or a conflicting write, is finished or undone from the record, and a resume goes back to the exact replica count
- The `napwake.dev/ignore` label, a protected namespace, and deleting the ManagedWorkload all hand back a paused workload, scaled up and released from KEDA, and remove its doorman routing. A workload whose target is deleted stops routing to the doorman
- KEDA: a ScaledObject's own `paused-replicas` value is put back after a pause, and uninstalling KEDA, or deleting the ScaledObject, while a workload is paused no longer blocks its wake
- A forecast veto no longer holds back a pause already decided, and doorman routing problems never hold up a pause or a wake: they're retried with backoff from 5 seconds to 5 minutes
- A dependency in a namespace outside `watchNamespaces`, or that can't be read, is reported (`DependencyNotFound`, reason `DependencyNotVisible`) and never blocks; `waitForReady` doesn't wait for a dependency that can't start (unmanaged at zero replicas); learned dependencies never form a cycle that holds both workloads awake; and a dependency learned from a wake gets a valid status
- Activity is written to status promptly enough that a restart can't pause a workload early
- Time an activity source couldn't be read no longer counts as idle time: a source out for longer than a couple of checks restarts the clock (`unobserved`), while a shorter blind spot, such as the minute before a woken workload's pods have metrics, leaves the last activity as the wake, so a workload whose use shows only in a Prometheus query isn't paused the moment Prometheus comes back from an outage, and the same holds for CPU

- A slow or unreachable Prometheus can't hold up wakes: the operator keeps one client for it, gives each query 2 seconds and a workload's queries 5 together, and once Prometheus fails, or times out on three queries in a row, doesn't ask it again for a minute, failing every query at once instead, with the error that failed. The condition message is the same whether a check asked Prometheus or not, so an outage is one status write per workload, not one a minute, and it never shows another workload's query

- Workloads with an injected sidecar, such as an Istio or Linkerd proxy, can pause. CPU usage was counted for every container in the pod but measured against requests from the pod template, which doesn't include injected sidecars, so the proxy's background work alone could keep a workload above the CPU threshold. Its pods used to leave the Service's endpoints before the doorman's EndpointSlice was in place and picked up, for 3 to 5 seconds a pause, about one refused request a pause under steady use. The doorman now fronts the Services beside the pods from the moment the pause starts, passing what it gets to a Ready pod, and the pods stop only once proxies send to it too. A pod that exits the moment it gets `SIGTERM` can still refuse a connection in the second before kube-proxy drops it, as in any rollout. A `preStop` sleep closes that
- A request held while the doorman's cache didn't yet show its workload Paused is stamped, and wakes it. It was held unstamped until `maxWait`, and the workload stayed asleep
- `kubectl napwake nodes` says why a node whose node group is at its minimum stays, instead of giving steps to unblock it that would save nothing. It reads each node group's sizes from Cluster Autoscaler's `cluster-autoscaler-status` ConfigMap, GKE's included, in the YAML of Cluster Autoscaler 1.30 and later or the text before. Karpenter keeps no minimum
- On GKE Autopilot, `scan` and `status` no longer say the bill drops only as nodes scale down. Autopilot bills what pods request, so it drops as pods stop
- With no node autoscaler found, `scan` and `status` say the bill won't drop until something removes nodes. They no longer say nothing will
- The scan report's Workloads section mentions what pausing freed and saved only once something is live
- The scan's headline says what was freed "pausing 1 live workload and others before they were protected", in place of "and workloads since protected"
- A click on **Wake it** that the browser says came from another address no longer gets "That page had expired", which asked for another click that couldn't help. That's a click from another site, or, over plain HTTP, an `Origin` that isn't the page's, as behind a proxy that rewrites `Host`. The page says the click came from another address, and how else to wake it, as a `403`
- The waking-up page draws its own tab icon, so browsers no longer ask the doorman for `/favicon.ico`, which it held until the workload was Ready. Its Content-Security-Policy allows its own style, no script, and images only from `data:`

## [0.1.7] - 2026-04-08

### Fixed

- Reconciler now initializes the idle detector and scaler in `initDefaults`; without these, the first reconcile of any workload with an `IdlePolicy` or `ScalePolicy` panicked with a nil-pointer dereference in production (#10)
- Cost status is now persisted after `accumulateCost`; previously the function mutated `Status.Cost` in memory but no caller called `Status().Update`, so cost data was silently dropped every reconcile
- First-pause resource snapshot is no longer lost; `Status.Pause` is now initialized before the snapshot is assigned, restoring the baseline used for savings calculations
- `resolveIdleAction` defensively nil-checks `IdlePolicy` to prevent the same class of panic from resurfacing via future callers
- Monthly cost reset now compares year and month so cross-year boundaries or backward clock jumps roll the bucket over instead of appending to the prior month
